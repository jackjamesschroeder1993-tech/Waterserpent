Wantirna South Was Built Across the Earthenware-to-PVC Changeover — and That Decides How Your Drain Behaves

Wantirna South was orchards and a research station until the City of Knox was carved out of the Fern Tree Gully shire on 16 November 1963. Residential settlement at Studfield began in the 1960s — Studfield Primary opened in 1965 with fewer than 200 pupils and had more than 650 by 1969, which tells you how fast the Knox City housing estate went up — and the Knox City Shopping Centre opened on 9 November 1977 on the corner of Burwood Highway and Stud Road. The suburb kept filling through the 1980s, the decade in which Knox built more homes than any other municipality in Melbourne, and the last big parcel, the 56-hectare Harcrest estate on the old quarry off Stud Road, only opened in 2014. The 2021 census counted 20,754 people in 7,895 dwellings, 77.6 per cent of them separate houses.

For a drain plumber, that timeline matters more than the street names. Houses from the 1960s and early 1970s in Studfield and around the Knox City estate were generally plumbed in earthenware — short lengths of glazed clay pipe with a joint every metre or so, which fifty years of ground movement and garden roots have been working on ever since. From the mid-to-late 1970s PVC took over, and the 1980s courts and the Harcrest blocks are almost all solvent-welded plastic. Earthenware blocks with roots at the joints and sometimes drops where a section has settled. PVC very rarely has root trouble but blocks with wipes, kitchen fat and the odd crushed section where a driveway went in on top of it. Same kit for both; different nozzle, different pressure, different advice at the end — and the first thing we do on a Wantirna South job is work out which era of pipe we are dealing with.

Before the Van Arrives — Five Things Worth Doing

Most of the mess from a blocked drain is made in the half hour after it is noticed, not by the blockage itself. Short version: stop adding water, and go and look outside.

  1. Stop using water anywhere in the house, and tell everyone else to. A blocked sewer holds a fixed volume, and every litre lifts the level toward the lowest opening — in a 1970s Wantirna South house that is usually the floor waste in the bathroom or laundry.
  2. Kill any appliance mid-cycle. A washing machine pump delivers a big slug of water in seconds, and the laundry is often the lowest fixture in the house.
  3. Find the overflow relief gully. On these estate blocks it is normally a grated dish in the side or back yard, often buried under a garden bed or a deck built over it in the 1990s. If it is weeping, the sewer is blocked downstream of it and it is doing exactly what it is there for. Do not seal it.
  4. Look for standing water around the slab edge and under the downpipes. Most of Wantirna South is slab-on-ground brick veneer. A broken stormwater line next to a slab can be softening the clay under the footing for months before a crack shows in the brickwork.
  5. Leave the chemicals on the shelf. Caustic cleaner does nothing to roots, nothing to a wad of wipes, and nothing good to a fifty-year-old clay joint. Our guide on whether drain cleaner works covers why.

The Wantirna South Year, Season by Season

🍂 Autumn

Fifty-year-old street trees and garden plantings drop their leaves into gutters and stormwater pits. A downpipe line that was half full of silt all summer blocks completely at the first bend. March is the cheap month to have the stormwater jetted, before the leaves and before the rain.

🌧️ Winter

The Blind Creek catchment’s flood record is a winter record — July 1987, June 1989, July 1991, July 1995. Long steady rain removes the spare capacity a half-restricted stormwater line was quietly relying on, and a wet patch beside the slab turns into a damp wall.

🌱 Spring

Root growth restarts. Any 1960s–70s sewer cleared over winter without sealing the joint the roots came through tends to close over again now, in the same metre of pipe as last time.

☀️ Summer

Short violent storm cells, and the February 2011 event that VICSES still describes as a significant flood for Wantirna South. A stormwater line that coped all year shows itself in twenty minutes when 50 millimetres lands in an hour on a flat clay block.

Warning Signs on a Wantirna South Property

  • One fixture answering another — flush and the basin burps, run the bath and the shower rises. Our guide on gurgling drains explains what is happening.
  • The same puddle on the driveway or beside the slab after every ordinary rain — repetition is the diagnostic. Big storms move about; a restriction stays put.
  • An outdoor grate lifting or overflowing — the overflow relief gully operating as designed. Urgent, not cosmetic.
  • A drain that was “fixed” last year doing it again — the symptom was cleared, the cause was not. Our guide on why drains keep blocking is written for exactly this.
  • A sunken strip of lawn or a dip in the paving along the sewer line — on a clay block that often means a joint has opened and the ground is being washed into the pipe.
  • A damp patch at the base of a brick veneer wall that never dries — a stormwater or sewer leak beside the slab, not rising damp.

Stormwater or sewer, earthenware or PVC, and where the line opens up. Inspection openings on these blocks are frequently under a 1990s deck, a concrete driveway or a garden bed, so this is often a locating exercise first.

2️⃣ Match the equipment to what is actually in there

3️⃣ Run the camera over the cleared length

Once water is moving, the self-levelling colour camera goes down with its distance counter and records the finished bore plus the pipe either side of the trouble. On a pre-1975 line that condition report is worth more than the clear.

4️⃣ Say plainly what it showed

An open joint at 8.4 metres, a belly holding water, a root mass the size of your fist — whatever the camera found gets described straight, with a PDF of screenshots and distances, and the video on request. Relining is the answer when leaving it is not.

When a Wantirna South Line Needs Relining, Not Clearing

If the camera shows roots coming through the same two or three joints of a 1960s earthenware sewer every year, clearing it again is a subscription, not a repair. A liner cured inside the existing pipe seals those joints, restores a smooth bore and leaves the driveway, the carport slab and the garden exactly as they were — which on an estate block where the sewer runs under the side concrete is the whole point. It is quoted in writing from the footage, never estimated blind. PVC lines rarely need it; when they do it is usually a crushed or bellied section, and our relining versus replacement guide explains when digging the short section out is actually the cheaper, better answer.

Blind Creek, Dandenong Creek and the Wantirna South Drain — Where Your Water Goes

Wantirna South sits in the angle between two creeks. Dandenong Creek runs down the western edge of the suburb in the valley EastLink now shares with it, 53 kilometres from its springs near Olinda to the Patterson River, and the Wantirna South Drain discharges into it near Patterson’s Bridge on the Dandenong Creek Trail. Blind Creek comes down from the Dandenong Ranges through Boronia and Ferntree Gully, passes Lewis Park, and joins Dandenong Creek on the suburb’s south-western corner. Immediately north of Westfield Knox the Blind Creek Trail runs along the wall of a Melbourne Water retarding basin — the “flood basin” behind the Knox City Tennis Club that locals know — which is there precisely because the creek cannot carry a big storm on its own.

Knox City Council draws the line between its drains and Melbourne Water’s at a catchment of 60 hectares. Below that, in the courts and local streets, the pits and pipes are Council’s, designed for a 5-year storm in residential areas. Above it — Blind Creek, Dandenong Creek, the retarding basins and the main drains — they are Melbourne Water’s, designed for the 10-year to 100-year events. Your private stormwater runs into the bottom of that system. The practical point is that when the Blind Creek basin is full and the Council pit at your kerb is backing up, your drain cannot discharge no matter how clean it is. That is a capacity problem, which nobody fixes in an afternoon, not a blockage, which we do — and the camera is what tells the two apart.

Knox’s Flood Record, and Where Wantirna South Sits in It

Being straight about this: Wantirna South is named. VICSES publishes a Blind Creek Catchment at Knox Local Flood Guide and lists it as relevant to residents of Ferntree Gully, Bayswater, Boronia, Wantirna and Wantirna South, with the municipal page stating that “locations at risk of flooding include Boronia, Bayswater, Wantirna, Wantirna South and Ferntree Gully”. The recorded events are September 1984, July 1987, June 1989, July 1991, July 1995 and February 2011, described as both riverine and flash flooding. Four of those six are winter floods, which is unusual for Melbourne’s east and tells you something about how a flat clay catchment behaves once it is saturated.

Council’s own position is clear and worth reading before you ring them about water in the garage: Knox provides flood protection for a 5-year storm in residential streets, aimed at habitable floor areas — gardens, yards, garages and sheds are explicitly excluded — and has “no legal responsibility to maintain, repair or inspect household drainage”. If your property is inside Council’s flood modelling you can order a Flood Information Report through ePathway (a statutory fee set by the State Government, ten working days) giving the 1% AEP flood extent, levels and depths for your block. None of this makes Wantirna South a floodplain. The low ground along Blind Creek and Dandenong Creek is parkland and retarding basin for good reason, and most of the housing sits above it on the clay rise. It does mean that a half-blocked stormwater line here has less margin than it would on a hill, and that the cheap time to find out is a dry week in March.

From Orchards to the Knox City Estate — What Is Under the Streets

The Bushy Park cattle run was taken up on the track to the Dandenongs in 1841, and the district settled into orchards after the Ringwood to Upper Ferntree Gully railway opened in 1889. Wantirna South was recognised as a locality when a mail service began in 1928; the post office opened in 1933 and the primary school in 1940. In 1956 an agricultural research station opened here, with an experimental station named for Sir George Knox, the local member and orchardist whose name the whole municipality took when it was severed from Fern Tree Gully on 16 November 1963 — Wantirna’s voters supported the split 89 per cent, the highest of any district polled. The Wantirna drive-in ran on Mountain Highway among the orchards from 1968 to 1984.

The eMelbourne history of Knox is blunt about what happened next: cheap land and unsewered, unmade-road subdivisions drew buyers on modest incomes, the population doubled in seven years from 1963, and in the 1980s Knox built more homes than any other municipality in Melbourne. Studfield went up first, from 1965; the Knox City Shopping Centre followed on 9 November 1977, roughly doubled in 1989–90, added its eastern section in October 2002 and became Westfield Knox in 2012; EastLink opened through the Dandenong Creek valley on 29 June 2008; and Harcrest added 840 lots on the Stud Road quarry from 2014. That history is why the drains here come in two kinds: 1960s–70s earthenware in Studfield and the first Knox City courts, and PVC under almost everything built after about 1980. Knowing which one you have is half the diagnosis.
