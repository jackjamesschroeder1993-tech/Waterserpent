The Township the Yarra Built

Yarra Street follows the river, and that is the shape of the place in one line. Warrandyte grew along the bank rather than out from a station, and almost everything about its water follows from that: land that falls hard toward the river, a main street sitting close to the flood plain, and reserves running along the water in both directions. Warrandyte State Park takes in an eight kilometre riverside reserve that reaches out to Wonga Park, along with Pound Bend and Whipstick Gully.

It is also where Victorian gold started. About two kilometres south of the township is Andersons Creek, described as "the site of the first gold discovery in Victoria in 1851, for which a government reward was paid", marked today by a cairn in Gold Memorial Road. Two decades later the miners went at the river itself: in the 1870s a tunnel was driven through rock at Pound Bend to divert the Yarra and expose the river bed for working. It is a useful reminder that people have been redirecting water through this valley for a very long time, and that the ground under a Warrandyte block has often been disturbed before.

The town's own record of high water goes back nearly as far. A bridge over the Yarra was washed away in a big flood in 1863 and took twelve years to replace. The township was flooded again in 1934. Fire has taken more, with 168 houses lost in the 1939 Black Friday bushfires and three houses destroyed in 2014. Between those events the suburb filled in steadily rather than suddenly, going from 1,085 residents in 1966 to 3,711 in 1976 and 5,520 by 2011.

For a plumber that history matters in a practical way. Warrandyte was not laid out in one decade to one standard. Drainage here has been added a property at a time over sixty years of building, on ground that ranges from river flat to genuinely steep, which is why we bring a camera rather than an assumption.

Manningham's Rules: Your Legal Point of Discharge

Manningham calls it the legal point of discharge, or POD, and in its written policy the Nominated Point of Drainage Discharge. The idea is simple enough: rainwater falling on your land is collected and taken to the lowest usable point, and that point is the handover. Council defines it as "the nearest pit or drain of adequate depth and capacity" or, where that is unclear, "a point specified by a council officer."

Then comes the sentence that matters most to a Warrandyte owner, and it is stronger than anything the surrounding councils publish: "The property owner is responsible for the construction and maintenance for all drains, including drains outside of the property boundary, up to the Council's stormwater system." Read that carefully. Not up to the fence. Not up to the kerb. Up to the council system, wherever that happens to be.

Council's long-standing drainage policy also fixes what a POD may consist of. Four options are recognised: a pit or drain owned by Council within an easement or road reserve, a swale drain sitting in a road reserve, a Melbourne Water drain, or a natural watercourse. Soakage and rubble pits are ruled out as a discharge point on urban land, small allotments and steep terrain, which removes a shortcut that gets suggested surprisingly often on sloping ground. Larger holdings are treated differently: above 1000 square metres, and expressly "including allotments located east of Mullum Mullum Creek", a soakage arrangement can be considered where the overflow is satisfactory, with the owner potentially having to show there is land for it on top of whatever a septic system already needs.

📏 The design storm

Manningham states its standards in AEP rather than ARI. The underground network is designed for storms with a 20 per cent chance of occurring in a year. On-site detention is designed on 20% AEP for discharge and 10% AEP for storage.

🌊 Where Melbourne Water takes over

Catchments greater than 60 hectares are Melbourne Water's rather than Council's. On a river-edge suburb that boundary comes up more than you would expect.

🏛️ Where to ask

Manningham Civic Centre, 699 Doncaster Road, Doncaster, on (03) 9840 9333. That is the number for a POD enquiry, a council drain report or a drainage plan request.

Council is also candid about why the older parts of the municipality behave the way they do. It attributes most flooding problems to older Manningham suburbs having been built to design standards that no longer apply, and points out that development from before the mid to late 1970s carried no obligation to work out where water would travel across the ground once the pipes were full. Overland flow was simply not part of the brief when much of Warrandyte was laid out. It happens regardless, and on this terrain it happens quickly and downhill.

What Helps Before We Arrive

  1. Stop putting water in. Every litre that goes down has to come out at the lowest opening available, and indoors that is usually a shower base or a floor waste rather than the fixture you noticed first.
  2. Do not start an appliance. A washing machine empties a large volume in one push, and on a restricted line that push is what takes it over the edge.
  3. Check the alarm panel if you are on the pressure system. Sounding points at Yarra Valley Water's equipment. Silent, with drains still misbehaving, points at your own pipework and at us.
  4. Find the grated fitting nearest the house and clear the top of it. That is the overflow relief gully, deliberately set lower than every fixture indoors so a surcharge lifts out there instead of in a hallway. Pot plants and mulch on top of it defeat the whole design.
  5. Skip the supermarket drain products. They will not move roots or set grease, and they leave a line full of caustic liquid that has to be dealt with before anyone can safely open it.

The wastewater network here belongs to Yarra Valley Water on 13 27 62, including the pressure sewer tank, pump, panel and boundary valve on connected Warrandyte properties. See blocked sewers.

🌧️ Stormwater — City of Manningham

🏠 Everything before that — you

Council's wording covers all drains up to its system, "including drains outside of the property boundary". On a Warrandyte block that can mean a long private run you never realised you owned.

The practical difficulty is that on large, sloping, heavily planted blocks the point of discharge is frequently the thing nobody can find. It may sit well inside the boundary rather than at the kerb, and after decades of garden beds, retaining walls and driveway work it is often buried or surfaced over. Nobody can fairly allocate responsibility for a blockage while that point is still missing. We locate it, put the camera on it, and from there the ownership question answers itself.

Warning Signs on a Warrandyte Property

  • A basin that gurgles when the toilet is flushed — two fixtures sharing one restricted line. See why drains gurgle.
  • The shower filling while the toilet is used — the shower is simply the lowest opening indoors reporting a shared-line fault.
  • The outside grate lifting or spilling in a storm — the overflow relief gully doing its job. Treat it as urgent, especially on the lower streets.
  • Run-off crossing the surface instead of entering a pit — usually a pit or outlet that has silted up, so the water simply goes around it and continues downhill.
  • The same stretch of drain failing twice — a defect with an entry point, not bad luck.
  • Ground that stays damp through a dry spell — often a leaking line feeding it, and on a septic property worth investigating quickly.
  • Odour that turns up on some days and not others — our guide on why drains smell explains the mechanism, and kitchen sink draining slowly covers the kitchen version of it.

When Relining Beats Digging on a Warrandyte Block

Excavating on ground that falls toward a river is a different proposition from opening a flat suburban lawn. There is the slope itself, the vegetation, the frequent presence of an above-ground pressure sewer discharge line to work around, and the plain fact that a trench across a steep block becomes a watercourse the moment it rains.

Relining avoids all of it by working inside the pipe that is already there. A resin-carrying liner is drawn or inverted into the failed length, pressed out hard against the old wall and left to cure, so the splits and open collars end up behind one continuous internal surface with nothing for roots to enter through and nothing for grit to catch on. The garden, the driveway and the slope stay exactly as they are.

It is not always the answer. A length that has genuinely collapsed, or that has dropped so far out of grade that water stands in it, wants replacing rather than lining, and the camera footage is what settles that question rather than a guess. We will also say when clearing on its own is genuinely enough. See pipe relining, or the comparison of relining against replacement.

Warrandyte Went on Pressure Sewer in 2012 — and 68 Properties Still Have Not

Warrandyte was a backlog sewerage area, meaning it was built and occupied long before any sewer reached it. Yarra Valley Water fixed that in 2012, and the reason it gives for the design chosen is worth quoting because it describes the ground precisely: the works went in as an onsite pressure sewer system because "The onsite septic systems were not performing due to the slope of the land and sub-soil conditions".

The engineering answer to that slope was squat tanks with above-ground discharge lines, a configuration chosen specifically for steep terrain where a conventional buried gravity network is impractical. The environmental result was substantial: the scheme removed 186.4 million litres of septic and greywater discharge from the environment per annum. Sewer was declared available to properties across 2013 and 2014.

More than a decade on, Manningham's Onsite Wastewater Management Plan 2025 to 2030 records exactly where Warrandyte landed. 444 properties are connected, which is 87 per cent. 68 are not. Of those, 33 hold their waste in onsite containment and 35 are still discharging raw sullage or treated effluent to stormwater.

So there are three different kinds of property in one small township, and they fail in three different ways.

⚙️ On the pressure system

The tank, pump, control panel and boundary valve belong to Yarra Valley Water on 13 27 62. An alarm means their equipment is not shifting the contents. Slow fixtures with a silent panel means the waste is not getting to the tank, and that pipe is yours.

🛢️ Onsite containment

Waste is held rather than dispersed, so the whole arrangement depends on the collection line staying clear and the pump-out schedule being kept. A blockage here does not just slow the house down, it fills something.

💧 Still discharging to stormwater

The 35 properties in this group have wastewater and rainwater meeting somewhere they should not. It is the group with the most to gain from a camera survey, because the connection points are usually undocumented.

If nobody has ever shown you which of the three you own, that is normal and it is fixable. Locating the tank, the boundary valve and the drainage runs takes an hour and it is the piece of information that decides who you ring the next time something stops.
