Blackburn Floods the Flash Way, Not the River Way

The City of Whitehorse draws a distinction that is worth carrying around in your head, because it decides what is worth doing about it. Council defines the first kind this way: "Flash Flooding is caused by a large amount of rain falling in a small area that causes storm water drains to overflow." The second: "Riverine Flooding is where water flows above the banks or sides of the stream or river." And then the sentence that settles which one applies here: "Flooding in the City of Whitehorse is usually flash flooding."

Riverine flooding is a landscape problem. Flash flooding is a drainage problem wearing a weather costume. It happens when more water arrives at a drainage system than that system can move in the time available, and the first place it shows up is wherever capacity is already reduced. A silted pit, a partly collapsed outlet, a downpipe line half full of debris. All of those sit comfortably inside a residential boundary, and all of them are fixable in an afternoon.

Council is also blunt about the worst outcome, and this is the part people find out about the hard way: "Flooding may cause sewage to overflow inside your home. Contaminated areas must be cleaned and disinfected." That is not a stormwater sentence. It means that in a big enough event, the two networks under this suburb can produce a result inside the house that is a health issue, not merely an inconvenience.

The sewerage authority for Blackburn, on 13 27 62. A separate network with separate assets. If waste is surcharging from an authority-side asset rather than from your own drain, this is who attends it.

🏠 The property owner

Every drain between your fixtures and the point where it leaves your land, whether it ends up in the stormwater system or the sewer. That is most of the pipework on the block, and statistically it is the part that blocks.

The two networks are meant to stay strictly separate, and one of the faults a camera turns up in older Blackburn properties is exactly the opposite: a downpipe, a yard grate or a surface drain connected into a wastewater line by somebody long gone. That connection behaves perfectly well in dry weather and then produces a sewer that surcharges every time it storms, which is precisely the scenario Council warns about. Drain smoke testing proves it, and our guide to who is responsible for a blocked drain covers the tenant, owner and agent side of it.

What to Do While You Wait

  1. Take the load off the system immediately. Taps off, showers off, and leave the washing machine and dishwasher alone until it is sorted. Both discharge a large volume in one burst, which is exactly what tips a marginal line over.
  2. Locate the lowest grated fitting outside near the building. That is the overflow relief gully, and it is set deliberately below the floor level of the house so that a surcharge escapes outdoors instead of through a bathroom. Anything sitting on top of it, or paving over it, defeats the one thing protecting your floors.
  3. In a storm, check surface pits and downpipe outlets. On a flash-flooding street this genuinely matters, because a covered grate takes nothing at all, and clearing one is a two-minute job with a rake.
  4. If sewage has come inside, treat it as contamination. Council's own guidance is that contaminated areas must be cleaned and disinfected. Keep people and pets out, photograph everything for the insurer, and do not start mopping before you have a record of the extent.
  5. Leave the caustic drain products on the shelf. They will not shift roots or set fat, and they leave a hazard in the line for whoever opens it next.

Signs a Blackburn Drain Is in Trouble

  • Water reaching the house during short intense downpours but not during long steady rain. That is the flash-flooding signature, and it usually points at capacity in your own line.
  • Sounds moving from one fitting to another. Our guide to gurgling drains explains the trapped air behind it.
  • The outdoor grate spilling. Urgent. The system is already surcharged and relieving itself where it was designed to.
  • Odour that changes with the weather rather than with the time of day. See why drains smell.
  • The same fitting blocking on a cycle, every spring or every wet spell. That is a defect, not coincidence.
  • A strip of unusually green lawn, or ground that has sunk in a line. Often traces a leaking pipe underneath.
  • A kitchen sink getting slower every month. Fat narrows the pipe from the wall inwards. See kitchen sink draining slowly.

Repeat Blockages: When a Blackburn Line Needs Relining Rather Than Clearing

There is a point where clearing the same drain again stops being maintenance. If a Blackburn line has closed over twice in the same spot, the pipe is the problem and the material inside it is only the symptom.

The repair is done from within. A resin-saturated liner is drawn through the failed length, pressed hard against the existing wall and cured in position, leaving a cracked barrel and a run of open joints all sitting behind one continuous new surface. Nothing above ground is opened up, which matters a great deal on Blackburn blocks where the drain commonly runs beneath a driveway, a crossover or a garden that has taken decades to establish. It also treats the actual failure. Clearing takes out what grew inside; a liner closes the doorway it grew through, and that doorway is the only thing deciding whether the problem returns. Twenty-Two Named Streets, and Two Named Drains

Blackburn gets more attention in the VICSES flood mapping than either of its bigger neighbours, and it is worth knowing exactly how much.

First, Blackburn appears on the list of Whitehorse suburbs most likely to be impacted, alongside Blackburn South, Box Hill South, Burwood, Burwood East, Forest Hill, Laburnum and Vermont. Box Hill and Nunawading are not on that list. That is not a criticism of Blackburn, it is simply a fact about where the modelling says water goes, and it is the honest difference between this suburb and the ones either side of it.

Second, Blackburn is covered by two separate flood maps in the guide, Blackburn Lake and Forest Hill and Laburnum and Blackburn. Neither Box Hill nor Nunawading gets that treatment.

The guide also names the drainage itself, which is unusual and genuinely helpful: "The Laburnum Street community is at risk from flooding along both the Blackburn North and South Parade Drains. Stormwater drainage at these locations may not be able to drain fast enough due the amount of rainfall causing them to overflow."

Read that carefully, because it describes a capacity failure rather than a blockage. Two named public drains that in a big event cannot move water away quickly enough. Now picture your own stormwater line trying to discharge into a system that is already at its limit. If your pipe is clear and correctly graded it will at worst back up briefly. If it is half full of silt, it has no chance, and the water finds the next lowest opening instead, which is usually a doorway.

The guide's account of what happened in February 2011 puts a location on it: "In February, a huge flow of water affected the east of Furness Park in Blackburn, and closed Norris Court off Naughton Grove."

Two practical points come out of all this. If you are on one of those streets, getting your own pits, downpipe lines and outlets cleared and camera-checked before the wet months is straightforwardly sensible. And if you are not on the list, do not relax too far. The list describes where public drainage is expected to overflow. It says nothing at all about whether the pipe running out of your own laundry is any good. See stormwater drains and the stormwater health check.

Blackburn Lake Is a Working Retarding Basin — and in 2024 the EPA Went Looking Upstream

Most people know Blackburn Lake as a sanctuary. Fewer know it is flood infrastructure with a job to do.

The lake was formed in the 1880s when the creek was dammed. Then came the part that matters: "In 1962 the dam wall on Lake Road was built by the Melbourne Metropolitan Board of Works to form a retarding basin to prevent flooding further down Gardiners Creek (Kooyong Koot)." The lake you walk around is holding water back deliberately so that properties downstream do not receive it all at once.

Where that water comes from is the interesting bit for a plumber: "Water flows into the lake from stormwater drains in the surrounding catchment area. As a result, chemicals, oils and other pollutants sometimes find their way into the lake." Wetlands have since been created along the two major stormwater inlets, and gross litter traps are installed to intercept what the drains bring down.

🔬 The 2024 investigation. In 2024 the Environment Protection Authority investigated persistent pollution in Blackburn Lake. Community members worked alongside the EPA to monitor the water, and the pollution was ultimately traced back to nine local businesses, who were then advised how to comply. Nobody had to dredge the lake to find the answer. The answer was upstream, in the drains. The EPA runs a 24-hour pollution hotline on 1300 372 842 if you ever see something entering a stormwater pit that should not be.

That case is the clearest possible demonstration of a principle we spend a lot of time explaining. A stormwater drain is not a bin with a lid on it. Whatever goes into a pit in a Blackburn street or a Blackburn car park arrives somewhere real, and in this suburb the somewhere is a public sanctuary at the bottom of the catchment. Paint rinse, workshop washdown, cooking oil tipped down a yard grate and detergent from a wash bay all end up in the same place.

It also cuts the other way. If your commercial premises in Blackburn has a stormwater system that is already choked with sediment and rubbish, it is both a blockage risk and a compliance risk, and one camera survey answers both questions at once. See commercial drains and CCTV drain inspection.

Where Blackburn's Water Actually Goes

The Gardiners Creek (Kooyong Koot) headwaters run through central Blackburn, and the reserves along that corridor are collectively known as the Blackburn Creeklands: Blacks Walk, Kalang Park and Furness Park, three continuous reserves totalling roughly 21 hectares and running about 1.4 kilometres from Middleborough Road to Blackburn Road. Furness Park was purchased by the City of Nunawading in 1941, well before most of the housing around it existed.

Historically, the watercourse about 400 metres south of the original township was called Blackburn Creek. That was the settlement-era name, and it is worth keeping separate in your mind from the Gardiners Creek naming used today rather than assuming the two labels describe exactly the same thing.

There is one more piece of drainage history here that most suburbs cannot claim. When the Blackburn Road level crossing was removed, drainage was written into the scope explicitly: "The Level Crossing Removal Project will deliver significant improvements to drainage in the area to remedy regular flooding of the Blackburn Station underpass." A station underpass that flooded regularly enough to need engineering out is a fair indication of how much water moves through the middle of this suburb during a storm.

A Model Township, a Water Engineer's Name, and a Century of Pipework

Blackburn's name may trace to James Blackburn, designer of Melbourne's Yan Yean water supply, which makes it one of the few suburbs in the east named after a water engineer. Given how much of its story is about moving water around, that is a pleasing coincidence.

The hotel opened in 1861 and the post office in 1876. The Box Hill to Lilydale railway arrived in 1882 and the place changed rapidly after that. The land boom of the 1880s here was driven by the Freehold Investment and Banking Company, which acquired thirty small farms and laid out a model township, "distinguishable by the triangular street design south of the railway station". The company built the public hall in 1888 and dammed the creek to form Blackburn Lake in 1889. The Depression of the 1890s then curtailed further development for years.

Growth resumed across the twentieth century, and the census tells the scale of it: 1,158 residents in 1911 and 12,796 by 2011. In 1959 a tree preservation society formed, "alarmed by the loss of tree cover in residential subdivisions", and that is the origin of the garden-suburb character the area is known for today.

For drainage, the relevant part is that Blackburn was plumbed in waves, over a very long period, on a street layout that was in places deliberately irregular. Older sections carry earthenware laid in short jointed lengths, and every one of those joints is a potential entry point once the ground has moved or the pipe has cracked. Later sections are PVC, which fails differently, usually through poor bedding, a dip, or damage from later works. The awkward spots on a camera survey are typically the junctions between one era and the next, where diameters change and connections were improvised.

That is also why a Blackburn drain that has blocked once often blocks again in exactly the same location. Clearing removes what got in. It does not close the gap it got in through, and only relining does that.
