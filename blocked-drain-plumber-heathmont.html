Why Drainage in Heathmont Behaves Differently

Most of the eastern suburbs shed water downhill without much argument. Heathmont is one of the places where that is not quite true, and if you have ever wondered why your drain seems to give up faster than your friend's place up the hill, this is the reason.

What to Do Right Now

If a drain in your Heathmont home has stopped, work through these before we arrive. They are ordered by how much difference each one makes.

  1. Stop adding water to the system. A blocked line is already holding everything you have sent it, and the next few litres are what decide whether it comes up in the yard or in the hallway.
  2. Hold off on the toilet if you can, and do not flush repeatedly to test it. Repeat flushing is the single most common way a nuisance becomes a mess.
  3. Check the overflow relief gully in the yard. It sits deliberately lower than any fixture indoors, which is how a backed-up line vents itself outside instead of through your house. If it is running, leave it running and keep the area around it clear.
  4. Think about the weather. On a low Heathmont block, a blockage plus a forecast downpour is a different problem from a blockage on a dry week — say so when you call and we will prioritise accordingly.
  5. Do not tip anything caustic down it. Supermarket drain products will not clear roots or settled silt, and they make the line unpleasant and unsafe to open.

When Heathmont Drains Block — The Seasonal Pattern

There is a rhythm to it, and on the low side of the suburb the wet months dominate the calendar more than they do elsewhere in Maroondah.

🍂 Autumn

The preparation window, and on this side of the municipality it is not optional. Every grate, downpipe line and yard pit takes on a season's leaf fall, and emptying them now is capacity banked for the wet months.

🌧️ Winter

Peak season on the flat ground. Saturated soil, high creek levels and a loaded receiving system leave your private line with the least help it gets all year.

🌱 Spring

Root growth ramps up and old defects reopen. Lines cleared in the wet months commonly close again by October if nothing was done about the hole the roots used.

☀️ Summer

The quiet season, and therefore the smart one for camera work, relining and anything you have been putting off while the weather has been against you.

Who Looks After Which Pipe in Heathmont — You, the Council, or Melbourne Water?

Heathmont is genuinely useful for explaining this, because all three levels of the system are visible within a couple of kilometres of each other: your own pipes, the City of Maroondah's street network, and a Melbourne Water waterway along the southern boundary. Work out which one you are standing on and the answer follows.

🏠 On your side of the line

Maroondah marks the boundary with a term you will see on its forms: the point of discharge, sometimes written as the Legal Point of Discharge. Your responsibility runs up to and including that point, which covers downpipes, yard pits, the gully and every metre of pipe under the lawn.

🛣️ The pipe in the street

Past that discharge point it becomes the City of Maroondah's stormwater network. Report a blocked or broken Council drain on 1300 88 22 33, which is answered 24 hours. For a private drain, the council simply says to contact a plumber.

🌊 The creek at the bottom

Dandenong Creek and Bungalook Creek are Melbourne Water assets, along with the retarding basins and the flood-warning gauge. That is a third owner again, and on creekside Heathmont blocks it is often the one your drainage ultimately reports to.

And then there is the sewer. Wastewater is not council business at all — Yarra Valley Water is the sewerage authority here and can be reached on 132 762. Keeping the two systems straight matters more than people expect, because a surprising number of older properties turn out to have a rainwater downpipe teed illegally into the sewer, or a cracked sewer quietly taking in groundwater. Both are found in minutes with a camera, and on low ground both make wet-weather trouble considerably worse.

Renting in Heathmont? Drains belong to the building, which puts the cost of repairing them with the landlord in most situations — the exception being a blockage that was obviously caused by something that should never have gone into the pipe. If you rent, email the managing agent so the request is dated and documented, keep a photo, and let them arrange the trade. If you own or manage the place, insist on seeing the camera recording: it is what makes the outcome fair and difficult to argue with. See our guide on who is responsible for a blocked drain.

⚠️ Worth doing before winter, not during it: on a low Heathmont block the useful question is not "is my drain blocked" but "how much of my drain is still open". Those are different questions, and only one of them can be answered by looking at a grate. A camera run through the stormwater line in summer tells you what capacity you will actually have in July, while there is still time to do something about it.

Signs Your Heathmont Drain Is Blocked

The obvious ones send people straight to the phone. The subtle ones are the reason we end up doing more excavation than we should have to.

  • One fixture reacting to another — the basin bubbles when the toilet goes, or the shower rises when the bath empties. See why drains gurgle.
  • Water taking noticeably longer to clear after every storm — a capacity problem building rather than a sudden blockage.
  • The yard gully surcharging — the overflow relief gully is protecting the inside of the house. Treat it as urgent, not as a nuisance.
  • Odour near the boundary or in the laundry — see why drains smell.
  • A drain that fails at the same time each year — that is a defect on a seasonal trigger, not coincidence.
  • Ground that stays soft in one strip long after everything else has dried — commonly a leaking line beneath it.
  • The kitchen waste slowing month by month — see kitchen sink draining slowly.

How We Clear a Blocked Drain in Heathmont

1️⃣ Work out the system first

Sewer or stormwater, one fixture or the whole property, and crucially on a flat block, where the line falls and where it discharges. The camera answers all three.

2️⃣ Clear to full bore, not just to flowing

On low-gradient ground a channel through the middle of a blockage is not good enough. 5000 PSI jetting takes the pipe back to its original diameter, which is where its capacity lives.

3️⃣ Prove it on camera

A second run once it is flowing, showing the cleared pipe and the condition either side of the problem. You keep the footage, which matters if a landlord, agent or insurer needs to see it.

4️⃣ Set out what comes next

Whether it will hold, roughly how long, and what would stop it recurring. Sometimes that is relining, sometimes it is a scheduled clean, and sometimes it is nothing at all.

Repeat Blockages: When Heathmont Pipes Need Relining, Not Clearing

Clearing a line twice is maintenance. Clearing it every year is a symptom the pipe is telling you about, and on flat ground it is worth listening to sooner, because the drain has no gradient in reserve to compensate for a defect.

Relining rebuilds the pipe from within, and the ground never has to be opened. A resin-carrying sleeve is drawn along the damaged run and held firm against the host wall while it sets, so what you finish with is one unbroken barrel where there used to be a string of jointed segments that roots could exploit. Lawns, driveways and paving are never touched. On a low block there is a second benefit that often gets overlooked: a smooth relined bore carries more flow at the same fall than a rough, jointed old one, which is exactly the margin a flat run needs.

Why Heathmont Sits in Maroondah's Highest-Risk Catchments

The Maroondah Flood Management Plan of May 2016, prepared with Melbourne Water, is unusually direct about it. That plan states that "the Dandenong Creek (Upstream of Boronia Road) and Bungalook Creek catchments have been rated as extreme flood risk. The extreme risk catchments cover parts of Heathmont, Ringwood East, Croydon South, Bayswater North and Kilsyth South." Those were the only two catchments in the entire municipality carrying an Extreme rating in that document. Bungalook Creek scored 9.00 out of 10, with 231 properties inside the one per cent AEP flood extent — 141 of them commercial or industrial and 90 residential.

📅 One honest caveat: those ratings come from the May 2016 plan, and that document itself flagged that Melbourne Water would be updating its flood risk ratings in due course. So the accurate way to put it is that these catchments were rated extreme in that plan, not that they carry that rating today. We would rather give you a dated fact than a confident one.

Flat Ground, Low Gradients and the Backwater Effect

The same plan makes a second point that matters more day to day than the risk rating does. It observes that most catchments across Maroondah are steep and undulating — but singles out an exception. The significant midland and lowland areas of the Bayswater and Croydon catchments, which take in Bungalook Creek and Little Bungalook Creek, are described as "relatively flat with low drainage gradients", and in those areas "it is more likely the major storm will cause flood due to the backwater effect" of the Melbourne Water drainage systems and waterways.

Backwater is worth translating out of engineering language, because it changes how you should think about your own pipes. A drain does not empty because it is a pipe; it empties because the far end has somewhere to put the water. When the creek or the trunk drain you ultimately discharge into is running high, the far end is effectively closed. Your water does not flow away — it queues, and then it looks for the next lowest opening, which is usually a gully in the yard or a floor waste inside.

The practical consequence for a Heathmont homeowner is this: your drain has less margin for error than the same drain would have elsewhere. A line that is thirty per cent restricted by roots or silt might never cause a symptom on a steep block in the ranges. Here, that same thirty per cent is the difference between coping with a downpour and not coping with it. Getting the private line back to its full internal diameter is not fussiness in this suburb, it is the main defence you have.

A creek on the boundary, and a gauge in the water

Dandenong Creek forms Heathmont's southern boundary, and Bungalook Creek meets Dandenong Creek within the suburb. Both are Melbourne Water waterways, not council drains, which matters when you are working out where a property's drainage ultimately ends up. Bungalook Creek also has retarding basins and wetlands built along it, at Canterbury Road and at the end of Tereddan Drive — storage designed to hold back a peak that would otherwise arrive downstream all at once.

There is one more detail that tells you how seriously this stretch of water is taken. Melbourne Water operates a level and rain flood-warning gauge on Dandenong Creek at Wantirna Road, Heathmont, and it is one of only six devices feeding Maroondah's flood warning system. A suburb does not get a warning gauge on its boundary by accident.

How Heathmont Was Built, and What That Means for the Pipes

Heathmont takes its name from the heath-like vegetation that once covered the rising ground here. The first shop opened in 1923, the railway station in 1926 and the post office on 15 February 1929, but the suburb stayed small for decades after that. Then it changed almost overnight. Victorian Places, the local history project run by Monash University and the University of Queensland, records the shift in one sentence: "From two shops and about 600 people in 1953 there were 28 shops and nearly 3000 people by 1958."

That is one of the sharpest short bursts of growth anywhere in the district. The primary school followed in 1952 and Heathmont East Primary in 1962, and the 2021 census counted 9,933 residents. Heathmont sat inside the City of Ringwood until the December 1994 amalgamation created the City of Maroondah.

Fast growth in a narrow window leaves a legacy underground: a lot of drainage of a similar age, installed to the standards of the day, now well past middle age. Earthenware drainage from that era is jointed rather than continuous, and once ground movement or a settled bedding opens one of those joints even slightly, roots have a way in. On a flat block, a partly root-filled line is also slower to self-scour, so debris that would wash through elsewhere tends to settle and stay.
