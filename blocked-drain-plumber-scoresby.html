Scoresby Was Swamp, Then Market Gardens, Then Suburb — and the Ground Still Remembers

The parish of Scoresby was surveyed in 1857 and named for William Scoresby, the Arctic navigator who had come to the colony the year before to study terrestrial magnetism. The Victorian Places history is candid about what the first buyers got: much of the land was swampy, because the creeks coming down off the Dandenong Ranges had no clear outfall across the flat, and it was only after that ground was drained that the market gardens went in. A village grew around the Ferntree Gully Road and Stud Road corner in the 1870s, and for the next eighty years the place grew vegetables — by the 1950s it was known as Victoria’s “brussels sprout capital”. A brick factory worked the local clay. The first market garden was subdivided for housing in 1959, and over the following thirty years the rest went the same way, as brick veneer on quarter-acre blocks spread south from Knox City into Scoresby and Rowville. The 2021 census counted 6,066 people in 2,211 dwellings, 96.3 per cent of them separate houses.

For a drain plumber that story has three practical consequences. First, the ground: clay that was wet enough to grow sprouts in is clay that swells in a wet winter and shrinks in a dry summer, and every house drain laid in it since 1959 has been moving with it. Second, the fall: Scoresby sits between Dandenong Creek on the west and Corhanwarrabul Creek on the east, and most blocks have very little slope to the kerb, so a stormwater line that has settled a few centimetres in the middle holds water and silt all year. Third, the pipe: a 1960s house here will have earthenware sewers with a joint every metre, a 1980s house will have PVC, and a lot of houses have both after an extension. Earthenware blocks with roots at the joints; PVC blocks with wipes, fat and crushed sections. The first job on any Scoresby call is working out which pipe, in which clay, we are dealing with.

Before the Van Arrives — Five Things Worth Doing

Most of the mess from a blocked drain is made in the half hour after it is noticed, not by the blockage itself. Short version: stop adding water, and go and look outside.

  1. Stop using water anywhere in the house, and tell everyone else to. A blocked sewer holds a fixed volume, and every litre lifts the level toward the lowest opening — in a Scoresby brick veneer that is usually the floor waste in the bathroom or the laundry.
  2. Kill any appliance mid-cycle. A washing machine pump delivers a big slug of water in seconds, and on a flat block it has nowhere to go but up.
  3. Find the overflow relief gully. On these estate blocks it is a grated dish in the side or back yard, often under a garden bed or a deck built over it later. If it is weeping, the sewer is blocked downstream of it and it is doing exactly what it is there for. Do not seal it.
  4. Look for standing water around the slab edge and under the downpipes. Almost all of Scoresby is slab-on-ground. A broken stormwater line beside a slab can be softening the clay under the footing for months before a crack shows in the brickwork.
  5. Leave the chemicals on the shelf. Caustic cleaner does nothing to roots, nothing to a wad of wipes, and nothing good to a sixty-year-old clay joint. Our guide on whether drain cleaner works covers why.

The Scoresby Year, Season by Season

🍂 Autumn

Sixty-year-old street trees and garden plantings drop into gutters and pits. On a flat block the leaf load settles in the low spot of the stormwater line and stays there. March is the cheap month to have it jetted, before the leaves and before the rain.

🌧️ Winter

Scoresby’s weather station has been recording since 1948 and averages 805.8 millimetres a year — wetter than the city because of the bay effect. Long steady rain saturates the clay, the creeks come up, and a half-restricted stormwater line loses the margin it was relying on.

🌱 Spring

Root growth restarts. Any 1960s–70s sewer cleared over winter without sealing the joint the roots came through tends to close over again now, in the same metre of pipe as last time.

☀️ Summer

Short violent storm cells. The Knox flood record includes February 2011, and a stormwater line that coped all year shows itself in twenty minutes when 50 millimetres lands in an hour on a flat clay block with nowhere for the water to run.

Warning Signs on a Scoresby Property

  • One fixture answering another — flush and the basin burps, run the bath and the shower rises. Our guide on gurgling drains explains what is happening.
  • The same puddle on the driveway or beside the slab after every ordinary rain — repetition is the diagnostic. Big storms move about; a restriction stays put.
  • An outdoor grate lifting or overflowing — the overflow relief gully operating as designed. Urgent, not cosmetic.
  • A drain that was “fixed” last year doing it again — the symptom was cleared, the cause was not. Our guide on why drains keep blocking is written for exactly this.
  • A sunken strip of lawn or a dip in the paving along the sewer line — on Scoresby clay that often means a joint has opened and the ground is being washed into the pipe.
  • A damp patch at the base of a brick veneer wall that never dries — a stormwater or sewer leak beside the slab, not rising damp.

How the Work Gets Done in Scoresby

1️⃣ Establish the system, the era and the access point

Stormwater or sewer, earthenware or PVC, and where the line opens up. Inspection openings on these blocks are frequently under a deck, a concrete driveway or a garden bed, so this is often a locating exercise first.

2️⃣ Match the equipment to what is actually in there

Roots in a 1960s earthenware line get a cutting head at a pressure the old clay can take. Fat and wipes in a PVC line get a flushing nozzle at full bore. A small-bore ensuite branch gets the compact electric jetter.

3️⃣ Run the camera over the cleared length

Once water is moving, the self-levelling colour camera goes down with its distance counter and records the finished bore plus the pipe either side of the trouble. On a flat clay block, how much water the line holds when it is “empty” is the number that matters.

4️⃣ Say plainly what it showed

An open joint at 6.2 metres, a belly holding 40 millimetres of water, a root mass — whatever the camera found gets described straight, with a PDF of screenshots and distances, and the video on request. Relining is the answer when leaving it is not.

When a Scoresby Line Needs Relining, Not Clearing

If the camera shows roots coming through the same two or three joints of a 1960s earthenware sewer every year, clearing it again is a subscription, not a repair. A liner cured inside the existing pipe seals those joints, restores a smooth bore and leaves the driveway, the carport slab and the garden exactly as they were. It is quoted in writing from the footage, never estimated blind. On Scoresby clay there is one honest caveat: relining fixes joints and cracks, but it does not lift a bellied section back to grade. If the camera shows a long low spot holding water, our relining versus replacement guide explains why digging that short section out and relaying it at the right fall is sometimes the better answer, and we will say so.

Dandenong Creek, Blind Creek and Corhanwarrabul Creek — Where Scoresby’s Water Goes

Scoresby is bounded by creeks on two sides. Dandenong Creek forms the western boundary, running down the valley it now shares with EastLink — 53 kilometres from its springs near Olinda to the Patterson River — with Melbourne Water’s stormwater treatment wetlands beside the tollway and the Dandenong Valley wetland shared path along the suburb’s edge. Blind Creek, down from the Ranges through Boronia, Ferntree Gully and Wantirna South, joins Dandenong Creek on Scoresby’s north-western corner opposite Jells Park. Corhanwarrabul Creek comes off the hills at Ferny Creek, runs down the eastern side of the suburb and meets Dandenong Creek at the Police Road Retarding Basin in Rowville. The eMelbourne history describes Scoresby simply as the suburb “between the Dandenong and Corhanwarrabul creeks”, and that is the right way to think about its drainage: a flat clay tongue of land with a creek on either side and not much fall to either.

Knox City Council draws the line between its drains and Melbourne Water’s at a catchment of 60 hectares. Below that, in the courts and local streets, the pits and pipes are Council’s, designed for a 5-year storm in residential areas and a 10-year storm in the industrial estates. Above it — the three creeks, the wetlands, the retarding basins — they are Melbourne Water’s, designed for the 10-year to 100-year events. Your private stormwater runs into the bottom of that system. The practical point is that when the creeks are up and the Council pit at your kerb is backing up, your drain cannot discharge no matter how clean it is. That is a capacity problem, which nobody fixes in an afternoon, not a blockage, which we do — and the camera is what tells the two apart.

Knox’s Flood Record, and Where Scoresby Sits in It

Being straight about the record: Scoresby is not named in a VICSES local flood guide. Knox has two — the Blind Creek Catchment guide, which covers Ferntree Gully, Bayswater, Boronia, Wantirna and Wantirna South, and an Upper Ferntree Gully guide — and the municipal page lists the floods of September 1984, July 1987, June 1989, July 1991, July 1995 and February 2011 as riverine and flash flooding in those northern suburbs. Scoresby’s creek frontages are parkland, wetland and industrial land rather than houses, which is partly why. We have found no published list of flood-prone Scoresby streets and will not invent one.

What Council does publish is its own position, worth knowing before you ring about water in the garage: Knox provides flood protection for a 5-year storm in residential streets, aimed at habitable floor areas — gardens, yards, garages and sheds are explicitly excluded — and has “no legal responsibility to maintain, repair or inspect household drainage”. If your property is inside Council’s flood modelling you can order a Flood Information Report through ePathway (a statutory fee, ten working days) giving the 1% AEP flood extent, levels and depths for your block. For most of Scoresby the honest answer is that the risk is not a creek breaking its banks. It is a flat clay block, a sixty-year-old stormwater line with a belly in it, and a summer storm — which is a drain problem, not a flood problem, and the cheap time to find it is a dry week in March.

From the 1857 Survey to EastLink — What Is Under the Streets

Allotments sold in 1857 made Scoresby a place as well as a parish. The village at Ferntree Gully and Stud Roads had its school by 1872 and its church by 1874, the cricket club was founded in 1879, and the post office opened on 20 January 1890 — it closed in 1979 and reopened in 1984, which says something about how quiet the place still was. By the 1930s there was a hall, a store and a motor garage. George Knox, the postwar orchardist and local member, gave the City of Knox its name when it was severed from Fern Tree Gully in 1963. After the war the Caribbean Boat Factory built a lake off Stud Road to test its boats, and Caribbean Gardens grew up around it as a pleasure garden and weekend market.

The first market garden was subdivided for houses in 1959 and the school moved to its present site in 1976 because the old one was overflowing. The eMelbourne history puts it in one line: over the following thirty years market gardens were replaced by suburban houses and industries, and Knox built more homes in the 1980s than any other municipality in Melbourne. The land near Caribbean Gardens went to industrial estates, and EastLink opened through the Dandenong Creek valley on 29 June 2008 with the stormwater wetlands beside it. The census tells the rest: 221 people in 1911, 333 in 1947, 5,835 in 2001 and 6,066 in 2021. That is the context for every Scoresby job: a 1960s-to-90s house on flat, heavy, ex-market-garden clay, with either earthenware or PVC under it depending on the decade — and the camera is how we find out which.
