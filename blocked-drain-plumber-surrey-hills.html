One Suburb, Two Councils — Which One Owns Your Street Drain?

Surrey Hills is the only suburb on this site where the first question on a stormwater call is which council are you in? The suburb runs from Whitehorse Road in the north to Riversdale Road in the south, and from Highfield Road across to Elgar Road — but the municipal boundary cuts through it. The City of Whitehorse holds the south-eastern wedge: south of Windsor Crescent, east of Warrigal Road, Canterbury Road and Alexandra Crescent, out to Elgar Road and down to Riversdale Road. Everything else is the City of Boroondara. Whitehorse lists Surrey Hills as “shared with City of Boroondara”, and historically the suburb was split the same way between the old Cities of Camberwell and Box Hill.

Why it matters: the two councils run their stormwater handover differently. Boroondara calls it the legal point of discharge, issues it through an online application by the owner or their builder or plumber for a non-refundable $167.50, and answers drainage calls on (03) 9278 4444 at any time. Whitehorse calls it a Stormwater Legal Point of Discharge Report, runs a three-stage report-plan-permit process, and has a customer line on 9262 6333 and a separate drainage line on 9262 6177. If you ring the wrong one about a blocked street drain you will be politely redirected and lose a day. For the sewer there is no split at all — Yarra Valley Water on 13 27 62 covers the whole suburb — and for anything inside your boundary neither council will attend, which is where we come in.

Before the Van Arrives — Five Things Worth Doing

Most of the mess from a blocked drain is created in the half hour after it is noticed, not by the blockage itself. Short version: stop adding water, and go and look outside.

  1. Stop using water anywhere in the house, and tell everyone else to. A stopped sewer holds a fixed volume, and every litre lifts the level toward the lowest opening in the building.
  2. Kill any appliance mid-cycle. A washing machine pump delivers a large slug of water in seconds. In a townhouse — a fifth of Surrey Hills housing is now semi-detached or townhouse — that slug may be coming from next door.
  3. Find the overflow relief gully. It is the grated opening outside, often near the laundry or kitchen wall. If it is weeping or overflowing, the sewer is blocked downstream of it and it is doing its job. Do not seal it or pave over it.
  4. Look for standing water along the side path and at the front boundary. The stormwater outlet to the kerb runs under the footpath, and on both sides of the council line that section is yours.
  5. Leave the chemicals on the shelf. Caustic drain cleaner does nothing to roots and makes an old pipe more dangerous to open. Our guide on whether drain cleaner works covers why.

The Surrey Hills Year, Season by Season

🍂 Autumn

The established street trees of the Union Road precinct drop their leaves together. Pits fill from the top down from March, and the downpipe lines under the canopy block at the first bend. March is the cheap month to empty them.

🌧️ Winter

Long steady rain removes the spare capacity a half-restricted stormwater line was quietly relying on. Damp at the base of a brick wall, or under a timber floor, starts turning up on the phone from June.

🌱 Spring

Root growth restarts. Any sewer cleared over winter without sealing the joint the roots came through tends to close over again now, in the same metre of pipe as last time.

☀️ Summer

Short violent storm cells on a suburb that is mostly roof, driveway and paving. The restriction that coped all year shows itself in twenty minutes, and the December storms of 2003, 2004, 2010 and 2016 are the ones in Boroondara’s record.

Warning Signs on a Surrey Hills Property

  • One fixture answering another — flush and the basin burps, run the bath and the shower rises. Our guide on gurgling drains explains what is happening.
  • The same puddle after every ordinary shower of rain — repetition is the diagnostic. Big storms move about; a restriction stays put.
  • An outdoor grate lifting or overflowing — the overflow relief gully operating as designed. Urgent, not cosmetic.
  • A drain that was “fixed” last year doing it again — the symptom was cleared, the cause was not. Our guide on why drains keep blocking is written for exactly this.
  • A green stripe in the lawn, or a bed that never needs watering — a cracked sewer feeding the garden.
  • A townhouse where the neighbours’ drains misbehave at the same time — a shared line, and a body-corporate question before it is a plumbing one. The camera shows where the shared section starts.

When a Surrey Hills Line Needs Relining, Not Clearing

Most of Surrey Hills was subdivided by 1892, with a second wave of building after the First World War, and the Union Road South precinct is heritage-listed for exactly that mix of 1880s–1910s and 1920s–40s houses. The drains under them are the same age. Clearing the roots restores flow; it does not close the joint they came through. When the camera shows the same open joint being re-entered year after year, a liner cured inside the existing pipe seals it for good, without a trench through the garden or the heritage frontage. Where the Water Goes — Faversham Road, West Creek and the Kooyongkoot

Surrey Hills sits on the northern side of Gardiners Creek — originally Kooyongkoot, “haunt of the waterfowl” — which rises in Blackburn, passes through Box Hill South, Burwood and Ashwood and reaches the Yarra at Hawthorn, more than thirty kilometres later. Boroondara’s own history notes that the creek’s tributaries “have been barrelled” and that the creek itself was straightened in the 1930s by sustenance workers; it is piped under R.H.L. Sparks Reserve in Box Hill. The Gardiners Creek Trail runs along the suburb’s southern edge.

Between your block and that creek sit Melbourne Water’s main drains. The Boroondara flood emergency plan names two that serve Surrey Hills: the Faversham Road Main Drain (Balwyn, Canterbury and Surrey Hills) and W Creek — the old West Creek — serving Camberwell, Canterbury, Glen Iris and Surrey Hills. Council’s street drains feed those, and your private pipes feed Council’s. The practical point for a Surrey Hills property is that the main drain your stormwater is heading for is a pipe with a design capacity, shared with thousands of other properties; when it is full in a big storm your private drain cannot discharge no matter how clean it is. That is the difference between a blockage, which we fix, and a capacity problem, which nobody fixes in an afternoon — and the camera tells the two apart.

Boroondara’s Flood Record, and Where Surrey Hills Sits in It

The Council’s flood mapping models the 1% AEP storm — flash flooding and overland flow from drains that cannot cope, not creek flooding — and flags affected properties as “subject to flooding under Building Regulation 153” on their Property Information Statements. Finished floors there must sit above the flood level plus 300 millimetres of freeboard; a flood level approval costs $310 and Melbourne Water takes flood enquiries on (03) 9679 7517. Boroondara’s 2026–27 budget commits $7.15 million to drainage renewal. On the Whitehorse side of the line, the Council does not publish a pipe design standard at all — a genuine contrast with Knox and Monash further east.

Being straight about the record: VICSES publishes local flood guides for Boroondara only for Glen Iris and Hawthorn, and Surrey Hills is not named in them. We have found no list of flood-prone Surrey Hills streets and no record of the suburb in the January 2024 event. The municipality’s headline floods are the 4 February 2011 storm, when 146 millimetres fell in 15 hours, the Gardiners Creek gauge reached 5.6 metres and the Solway footbridge was washed away, and December 2016, when the SES took more than 1,200 calls. Ashburton station flooded in 2004 and 2006. None of that makes Surrey Hills a floodplain. It makes it a suburb of very old private drains feeding piped creeks that fill up in the big events — which is exactly when a half-blocked line stops being an inconvenience.

From Knipe’s 1878 Subdivision to the 2023 Rail Trench — What Is Under the Streets

The land was part of Henry Elgar’s 1841 Special Survey. J.H. Knipe named it after Surrey in England when he subdivided south of Mont Albert Road in 1878; the railway came through from Camberwell in 1882, with a regular service to Surrey Hills station from 1 September 1883, and the post office opened on 1 October 1884. Most of the suburb had been cut into lots by 1892 — then the 1890s depression stopped everything, and the next building wave did not come until after the First World War. Water service reservoirs arrived around 1914 and the Riversdale Road tram in 1916. The population went from 2,069 in 1891 to 13,133 in 2011, and the 2021 census counted 13,655 people in 5,556 dwellings: 66.9 per cent separate houses, 20.5 per cent semi-detached or townhouses and 12.3 per cent flats, with 41.8 per cent of homes owned outright.

The heritage study for the Union Road South Residential Precinct — Union Road, Bona Vista Avenue, The Avenue and Warwick Avenue — describes exactly the two layers a drain plumber finds under the suburb: late Victorian and Federation houses of the 1880s to 1910s, and interwar houses of the 1920s to around 1940. The newest layer is the townhouse and dual-occupancy work of the last thirty years, which tends to tie new PVC into those original street connections. And the most recent change of all is the one everyone noticed: the Union Road and Mont Albert Road level crossings were removed by lowering the railway into a 1.3-kilometre trench, with the new Union Station opening on 22 May 2023 in place of the old Surrey Hills and Mont Albert stations. The early works for that project included stormwater drainage and watermain upgrades along Union Road between Croydon Road and Windsor Crescent — worth knowing if your Union Road stormwater connection has behaved differently since.
