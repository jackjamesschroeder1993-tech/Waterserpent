Canterbury Has the Oldest Drains in This Part of Melbourne — and That Changes How the Job Is Done

Canterbury’s post office opened in 1870, the railway arrived on 1 December 1882, and the Claremont Park Estate was subdivided in 1885. Monomeath Avenue was formed in 1900 and paved in 1911. The heritage study for the Maling Road precinct describes it as a “highly representative Victorian and Federation-era residential precinct” with “strong and well preserved interwar elements”, and the Balwyn Road precinct citation makes the point that “all periods are nearly equally represented”. For a drain plumber that is the whole brief in two sentences. A good share of the house drains under Canterbury were laid between the 1880s and the First World War, in short lengths of earthenware with joints that are now well past a century old, and they sit under some of the most established gardens in Melbourne.

Two practical consequences. First, pressure. A 5000 PSI jetter will cut roots out of a Victorian sewer cleanly, but only if the nozzle and the pressure are chosen for the pipe rather than for the brochure — old clay is cleared gently, with a flushing head doing most of the work and a cutting head used where the camera says it is needed. Second, access. On a heritage block the honest answer to a cracked drain is very often relining from the inside, because the alternative is a trench through a hundred-year-old garden or a bluestone path. The camera decides it; you see the footage before anyone quotes anything.

Before the Van Arrives — Five Things Worth Doing

Most of the mess from a blocked drain is created in the half hour after it is noticed, not by the blockage itself. Short version: stop adding water, and go and look outside.

  1. Stop using water anywhere in the house, and tell everyone else to. A stopped sewer holds a fixed volume, and every litre lifts the level toward the lowest opening — in an old house that is often a floor waste in a bathroom that was added in the 1970s.
  2. Kill any appliance mid-cycle. A washing machine pump delivers a large slug of water in seconds.
  3. Find the overflow relief gully. On a Canterbury block it can be a long way from the house, down the side path or near an old outhouse location. If it is weeping, the sewer is blocked downstream of it and it is doing exactly what it is for. Do not seal it.
  4. Look for standing water, and look under the house if you can. Timber-floored Victorian and Federation houses have subfloors, and a cracked drain can be wetting the ground under there for months before anything shows inside.
  5. Leave the chemicals on the shelf. Caustic cleaner does nothing to roots and nothing for a century-old pipe. Our guide on whether drain cleaner works covers why.

The Canterbury Year, Season by Season

🍂 Autumn

The avenues planted a century ago drop their leaves all at once. Pits fill from the top down from March, and the downpipe lines under deciduous canopies block at the first bend. March is the cheap month to empty them.

🌧️ Winter

Long steady rain removes the spare capacity a half-restricted stormwater line was quietly relying on. Rising damp at the base of a brick wall and a musty subfloor start turning up on the phone from June.

🌱 Spring

Root growth restarts. Any sewer cleared over winter without sealing the joint the roots came through tends to close over again now, in the same metre of pipe as last time.

☀️ Summer

Short violent storm cells. Boroondara’s flood record is a December record — 2003, 2004, 2010 and 2016 — and a restriction that coped all year shows itself in twenty minutes when one of those arrives.

Warning Signs on a Canterbury Property

  • One fixture answering another — flush and the basin burps, run the bath and the shower rises. Our guide on gurgling drains explains what is happening.
  • The same puddle after every ordinary shower of rain — repetition is the diagnostic. Big storms move about; a restriction stays put.
  • An outdoor grate lifting or overflowing — the overflow relief gully operating as designed. Urgent, not cosmetic.
  • A drain that was “fixed” last year doing it again — the symptom was cleared, the cause was not. Our guide on why drains keep blocking is written for exactly this.
  • A subfloor that smells, or a sagging section of timber floor — in a Victorian or Federation house, a cracked drain under the building is a structural problem before it is a plumbing one.
  • A garden bed along the sewer line that never needs watering — the sewer is watering it.

How the Work Gets Done in Canterbury

1️⃣ Establish the system and the access point

Stormwater or sewer, original house or extension, and where the line opens up. On a block that has been landscaped for a century the inspection openings are frequently under paving or lawn, so this is often a locating exercise first.

2️⃣ Match the equipment to what is actually in there

Roots in earthenware get a cutting head at a pressure the pipe can take. Grease gets a flushing nozzle. A small-bore bathroom branch gets the compact jetter. The age of the pipe sets the pressure, not the other way round.

3️⃣ Run the camera over the cleared length

Once water is moving the camera goes down and records the finished bore plus the condition of the pipe either side of the trouble. On a pre-1920 line that condition report is worth more than the clear.

4️⃣ Say plainly what it showed

An open joint, a dropped section, a tree root the size of your wrist — whatever the camera found gets described straight, including the cases where leaving it for now is the honest advice. Relining is the answer when it is not.

When a Canterbury Line Needs Relining, Not Clearing

This is the suburb where no-dig repair earns its keep. The Maling Road and Balwyn Road precincts carry heritage overlays, the gardens along the Golden Mile are the reason people bought the houses, and nobody wants a trench through either. A liner cured inside the existing earthenware seals the open joints the roots came through, restores a smooth bore and leaves the garden exactly as it was. It is quoted in writing from the camera footage — never estimated blind — and our relining versus replacement guide explains when the older pipe is genuinely past saving.

West Creek, Myrtle Road and Faversham Road — the Drains Under Canterbury

The Victorian Places history records Canterbury as “pleasantly well watered” by West Creek. You will not find West Creek on a street map now; it survives as a name in the Boroondara flood emergency plan, which lists W Creek as a Melbourne Water drain serving Camberwell, Canterbury, Glen Iris and Surrey Hills. The same list names the Myrtle Road Main Drain (Camberwell and Canterbury) and the Faversham Road Main Drain (Balwyn, Canterbury and Surrey Hills). Those three pipes are where Canterbury’s stormwater goes once it has left your block and Council’s street drains.

To the north-east, Back Creek rises as a spring on the hill at the corner of Canterbury and Elgar Roads in Box Hill, is piped underground as far as Union Road, runs open to Cornell Street and again from Camberwell Road to south of Toorak Road, and joins Gardiners Creek — originally Kooyongkoot, “haunt of the waterfowl” — at Nettleton Park in Glen Iris. Back Creek has its own flood emergency plan within Boroondara’s, and the plan notes that flooding on Gardiners Creek can back up into its lower reaches. For a Canterbury property the practical point is that the creek your stormwater is heading for is a pipe with a design capacity, shared with thousands of other properties. When it is full in a big storm, your private drain cannot discharge no matter how clean it is. That is the difference between a blockage, which we fix, and a capacity problem, which nobody fixes in an afternoon — and the camera is what tells the two apart.

Boroondara’s Flood Record, and Where Canterbury Sits in It

The Council’s 2016 flood mapping study covered every catchment in Boroondara and identified around 5,800 properties at risk of flash flooding — modelled on the 1% AEP storm, which means overland flow from drains that cannot cope rather than a creek breaking its banks. Properties inside that mapping carry a Special Building Overlay or a Land Subject to Inundation Overlay, both of which require a planning permit, and finished floors there must sit above the flood level plus 300 millimetres of freeboard. The flood assessment report is free; a flood level approval is $310. Council’s 2025–26 budget put $6.99 million into drainage renewal and the 2026–27 budget $7.15 million. Canterbury contributes 3,414 rateable properties to that.

Being straight about the record: VICSES publishes local flood guides for Boroondara only for Glen Iris and Hawthorn. Canterbury is not named in them, and we have found no published list of flood-prone Canterbury streets. The municipal plan’s historic floods are 1891 (the worst on record), 1914, 1934 (350 millimetres in 48 hours), 1974, December 2003, December 2004 (70 millimetres in about 90 minutes in Kew and Hawthorn), December 2010 and 4 February 2011, when 146 millimetres fell in 15 hours, the Gardiners Creek gauge reached 5.6 metres and the Solway footbridge was washed away. The December 2016 storm brought the SES more than 1,200 calls across the municipality. None of that makes Canterbury a floodplain. It makes it a suburb of very old private drains feeding piped creeks that fill up in the big events — which is exactly when a half-blocked line stops being an inconvenience.

From the 1882 Railway to the 1968 Embankment — What Is Under the Streets

Canterbury station opened on 1 December 1882 on the Camberwell–Lilydale extension and was named for Viscount Canterbury, Governor from 1866 to 1873. Maling Road grew up beside it: more than fifty Edwardian-style shops, most of them put up in the early 1900s, and the 1889 boom-style Canterbury Mansions hotel on the corner of Canterbury Road, which closed after the 1920 Local Option Poll and is now on the Victorian Heritage Register. The line was electrified in December 1922, and the station you use today is the elevated one opened on 15 September 1968, when the Canterbury Road crossing was grade-separated and the tracks raised about six metres. A 1970s proposal to demolish part of Maling Road for a supermarket was defeated, which is why the strip still looks the way it does.

The housing tells the same story in three layers. The 1885 Claremont Park Estate and the streets around Maling Road are Victorian and Federation; the Golden Mile — Mont Albert Road west from Balwyn Road and the avenues down to Canterbury Road, Monomeath Avenue above all — was laid out from 1900; and the interwar houses that the heritage study calls the third major phase filled in the gaps through the 1920s and 30s. The 2021 census counted 7,800 people in 3,176 dwellings, 64.5 per cent of them separate houses, with a median age of 46 and 47.8 per cent of homes owned outright — long-held houses, many of them on their original drains. That is the context for every Canterbury job: the pipe under the garden may be older than anyone who has ever lived in the house.
