First: Stop the Water (Don't Flush Again)

When a toilet blocks, the biggest mess doesn't come from the blockage — it comes from the second flush. The bowl can only hold one tank's worth of water. Flush again before it's cleared and that water has exactly one place to go: over the rim and onto your bathroom floor.

So step one is simple: hands off the button. If the water is sitting high, give it 10 minutes — a partial blockage will often let the level drop slowly on its own. If the bowl looks like it's about to overflow, take the lid off the cistern and push the flapper valve (the rubber seal at the bottom) closed, or turn off the small isolation tap on the wall behind the toilet. That stops any more water getting in while you work.

Method 1: The Plunger — Done Properly

A plunger is still the fastest way to clear a simple blockage, but most people use the wrong one, the wrong way. You want a flange plunger — the type with an extra rubber sleeve that folds out of the cup. That sleeve is what seals against the toilet outlet. The flat-bottomed sink plunger from under the laundry trough won't seal on a curved bowl.

Here's the technique:

1️⃣ Get a seal

Lower the plunger in at an angle so the cup fills with water, not air, then seat the flange right into the outlet at the bottom of the bowl. Water pushes blockages; air just compresses.

2️⃣ Push AND pull

Plunge firmly down and pull back up without breaking the seal, for 20–30 seconds. The pull-back matters as much as the push — it rocks the blockage loose in both directions.

3️⃣ Test gently

If the water suddenly glugs away, you've cleared it. Refill the bowl with a bucket of water first to check it drains before you trust the flush button again.

4️⃣ Know when to stop

Two or three rounds and no movement? Stop. More force from here risks pushing the blockage deeper into the pipe or damaging the pan seal — and it means the problem isn't in the bowl.

Method 2: Dish Soap & Warm Water (No Plunger Needed)

No plunger in the house? This is the method I'd give my own family, and it's kinder to your toilet than anything from the supermarket chemical aisle.

Squirt a good amount of dishwashing liquid into the bowl — a quarter of a cup is fine. Then slowly pour in a bucket of warm water from waist height. The height gives the water a bit of push, the soap lubricates the blockage, and the warmth helps soften packed toilet paper. Now walk away for 20–30 minutes and let it work. When you come back, the level has often dropped on its own — top the bowl up with the bucket to confirm it's draining before you flush.

One rule: never use boiling water. Porcelain doesn't like thermal shock, and a cracked toilet turns a $0 fix into a full replacement. Warm from the tap is all you need.

What NOT to Do

Half the blocked toilets I attend have been made worse by a DIY "fix" first. Skip these:

❌ Caustic chemical cleaners

They rarely shift a real blockage, they damage older pipes and seals, and they turn the bowl into a hazard for whoever ends up clearing it — which slows the job and adds to the cost.

❌ Wire coat hangers

They scratch the porcelain, they can jam in the S-bend, and they're too short to reach a blockage that's made it into the drain line anyway.

❌ Flushing "to see if it's fixed"

Test with a bucket of water instead. A flush commits a full cistern — if the blockage is still there, that's your floor gone.

❌ Ignoring a repeat offender

A toilet that blocks every few weeks isn't bad luck — something's wrong further down the line. Keep clearing the symptom and you'll keep paying for it. Find the cause once.

When It's Not the Toilet — Signs of a Deeper Blockage

Here's the part most guides skip: sometimes the toilet is just the messenger. If the real blockage is in your main sewer line, no amount of plunging will fix it, because the problem isn't in the bowl — it's metres down the pipe. The giveaways:

Other fixtures react. The shower gurgles when you flush, the floor waste bubbles, or the laundry backs up. Multiple fixtures = main line. There's a smell. A sewage odour inside or outside means wastewater is sitting in the pipe. The overflow relief gully is spilling. That grated drain outside near an external wall is designed to overflow before sewage comes up inside — if it's spilling or has paper around it, your sewer is blocked. It keeps happening. In Melbourne's east, a toilet that re-blocks every few weeks almost always means tree roots in the sewer line.

Any of those signs and it's time to stop DIY-ing. Keep It From Blocking Again

The three Ps rule is the whole game: pee, poo and (toilet) paper — nothing else goes down. Wet wipes are the number one toilet-blocker I pull out of Melbourne drains, and yes, that includes every brand stamped "flushable" — they don't break down like paper, they rope together in the pipe. Keep a bin in the bathroom for wipes, cotton buds and sanitary products, go easy on the half-roll-per-flush habit, and if your place is older and surrounded by gums, think about a preventative camera check before the roots decide for you.
