Why Melbourne's East Is Root Country

Three things have to line up for tree roots to take over a drain, and the eastern suburbs have all three. First, established trees — gums in the Ranges, liquidambars, elms and planes on the older streets below. Second, original earthenware (clay) pipes laid anywhere from the 1920s to the 1970s, joined in short sections with mortar joints that open up as the ground moves. Third, Melbourne's reactive clay soil, which swells every wet winter and shrinks every dry summer, working those joints looser year after year.

A root doesn't smash its way in. It finds a hairline crack or a weeping joint, sends in a feeler, and quietly builds a mesh that catches everything that flows past. By the time the toilet gurgles, there can be metres of root in the line. If you want the full story of how intrusion works, our guide to tree roots in drains covers it — this post is about where it happens most.

The Ranking — Where We Pull the Most Roots

🌲 1. The Dandenong Ranges — Kalorama, Montrose, Olinda

Our home turf, and still the root capital of the east. Mountain ash and messmate with root systems built to chase moisture down a hillside, hitting 50-year-old earthenware on spring-fed ground that never fully dries. If you're in Kalorama, Montrose or Olinda, an annual camera check genuinely is cheaper than the Sunday-night emergency.

⛰️ 2. The foothills line — The Basin, Boronia, Ferntree Gully

Everything the hill sheds — water, silt and roots chasing it — ends up here. Root intrusion plus stormwater silt is the classic Boronia combination, and it's why late winter is our busiest season along the bottom of the hill through The Basin and Ferntree Gully.

🌳 3. Croydon & Croydon North

Post-war streets, mature exotics, long sloping blocks on clay — Croydon gives us some of the highest repeat-blockage rates anywhere. Gorgeous gardens, merciless roots.

🍂 4. Lilydale & Mooroolbark

Where the Yarra Valley meets suburbia. Older stock near the town centres runs original clay lines under some seriously established trees — Lilydale and Mooroolbark keep the jetter busy all winter.

🏡 5. Blackburn & Nunawading

The "bush suburb" pockets around Blackburn Lake are on tree preservation streets with interwar pipes now 80–100 years old. Next door, Nunawading's old brickworks and clay-pit ground settled unevenly, and every opened joint is a root invitation. Blackburn earns its podium spot every winter.

🌿 6. The liquidambar belt — Mitcham, Vermont & Forest Hill

1950s–60s subdivisions where the street liquidambars are at their destructive prime — they're among the worst root offenders in Melbourne. We're in Mitcham, Vermont and Forest Hill most weeks through the cold months.

🏞️ 7. The creek corridors — Wantirna South & Scoresby

Younger housing stock means more PVC, which roots struggle with — but anything backing onto the Dandenong Creek parkland cops roots from the reserve side. Wantirna South and Scoresby punch above their weight for exactly that reason.

🏘️ 8. Ringwood & Heathmont

A mixed bag — renewed stock has newer drainage, but the established streets off Canterbury Road are classic clay-and-canopy territory. Ringwood roots tend to announce themselves in the first heavy rains of winter.

Keeping Roots Out

If you're planting near pipes, keep thirsty species — willows, liquidambars, figs — at least ten metres from any drainage line, and pick smaller natives closer in. If your street made the top half of this ranking and your house is pre-1980, assume earthenware until a camera proves otherwise, and put a yearly inspection in the calendar for late autumn — before winter rains turn a partial blockage into a flooded laundry. Frequently Asked Questions

Which suburbs in Melbourne have the worst tree root drain problems?

In our jobs, the worst tree root drain blockages cluster in the Dandenong Ranges (Kalorama, Montrose, Olinda), the foothills line through The Basin, Boronia and Ferntree Gully, and the older clay-pipe suburbs like Croydon, Blackburn, Nunawading, Mitcham and Vermont. Established trees plus original earthenware pipes is the common thread.

What trees are worst for blocking drains?

Liquidambars, willows, elms, plane trees and figs are the repeat offenders across Melbourne's east, with gums dominating in the Ranges. Species matters less than distance though — any thirsty tree within about ten metres of a cracked or jointed pipe can find its way in.

How much does it cost to clear tree roots from a drain in Melbourne?

Most blocked drains in Melbourne cost $300–$750 depending on the issue. A single-fixture clear starts around $300, while a root-bound main line needing hydro jetting plus a CCTV camera inspection runs $500–$750 depending on severity. You get a fixed price before any work starts.

Does root killer work instead of calling a plumber?

Copper sulphate foams kill the roots they touch, but the dead root mass stays in the pipe catching waste, and the crack the roots entered through is still open. It can buy you months at best. Cutting the roots out and sealing the entry point is the only permanent fix.

Are tree roots in my drain the council's responsibility?

On private property the drains are yours, even if the roots came from a council street tree. Councils rarely accept liability, but it's worth reporting with photos — we can supply CCTV footage of the intrusion point for your claim if you want to pursue it.

How do I know it's tree roots and not something else?

Blockages that keep coming back in the same spot, drains that slow down after dry spells, and gurgling toilets all point to roots. One camera pass settles it for certain — we show you the screen on the day and you see exactly what's in the pipe.
