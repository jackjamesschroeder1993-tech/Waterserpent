When a sink stops draining, the first thing most people reach for is a bottle of drain cleaner from the supermarket. Sometimes it helps. What Drain Cleaner Actually Does

Most supermarket drain cleaners — Drano and the like — are built around sodium hydroxide (caustic soda / lye), often mixed with a little aluminium and bleach. When it meets water it generates heat, and that heat plus the alkali is what breaks down grease, hair and soap scum. On a drain that's just running slowly because the pipe has narrowed with a bit of fat and gunk, it can dissolve enough of that layer to get things flowing again.

The key word there is flowing. Drain cleaner only works if it can actually reach the clog and the dissolved gunk can wash away past it. It's a maintenance product for a sluggish drain, not a blockage buster. The moment the water has completely stopped, or the problem is something solid the chemical can't dissolve, it stops being useful and starts being a liability.

It's also worth knowing what it can't touch. Drain cleaner does nothing to tree roots, which are the number-one cause of blocked drains across the Dandenong Ranges and Yarra Valley. It won't clear a collapsed or cracked pipe, a solid mass of wet wipes, or a blockage sitting metres down the main line. Those are jobs for mechanical clearing, not chemistry.

When It Can Help vs When It Can't

A quick guide to where a bottle of drain cleaner is worth a shot, and where it's a waste of money — or worse:

Completely blocked, water sitting—Pools as corrosive water, does nothing

The pattern is simple: if the water is still moving and it's a kitchen or bathroom drain, a careful single dose might get you going. If the water has stopped, or it keeps coming back, the cause is further down the line and no chemical is going to fix it. The Real Risks of Chemical Drain Cleaners

This is the part the label doesn't shout about. Used in the wrong situation, drain cleaner causes three problems that turn a small hassle into a bigger, more expensive one.

🔥 It can damage your pipes

The heat that dissolves grease also softens PVC and weakens old corroded metal. Repeated use, or a dose that sits in one spot, can warp joints and leave pipes brittle — a real risk in Melbourne's older homes on terracotta or aged metal lines.

🧪 It pools as a "toxic soup"

On a fully blocked drain the chemical has nowhere to go. It sits on top of the clog as a pool of highly corrosive standing water — doing no good, and now dangerous for anyone who touches that water, including the plumber.

☠️ Fumes and burns

Caustic soda burns skin and eyes, and mixing drain cleaners with bleach, other cleaners or acids can create dangerous fumes. Plunging a chemical-filled drain can splash it straight back at you. It's genuinely worth respecting.

💸 It can cost you more

A blocked drain we'd normally jet clear quickly takes longer and needs extra care once it's full of chemicals. Doubling up on doses that were never going to work just adds chemical damage on top of the original problem.

The single biggest mistake is pouring a second bottle in when the first did nothing. As one plumber's guide put it, that "doubles the chemicals in your pipes without improving your odds." If a careful first dose hasn't shifted it, the answer isn't more chemical — it's finding out what's actually down there.

What About Caustic Soda and the Baking-Soda Hack?

Two things people often try before calling. Caustic soda is the same sodium hydroxide, just in raw flake form and usually stronger. It can shift a grease clog, but it's a serious chemical — it burns skin and eyes, reacts with aluminium to give off flammable gas, isn't suitable for septic systems, and creates that same corrosive pool if the drain is fully blocked. For anything beyond a minor slow drain, most drainage experts will tell you the risk far outweighs the cost of having it cleared properly.

The baking soda and vinegar trick is the opposite problem — it's safe, but it barely does anything. The dramatic fizz is just a reaction producing water with a trace of salt in it; there's very little evidence it clears a real clog, and it does nothing to grease, roots or a solid blockage. Fine to try on a slightly sluggish drain, but don't expect it to rescue a blocked one. What to Do Instead

If the drain is just slow, there's plenty worth trying before you reach for chemicals at all — and it's gentler on your pipes. For a real blockage, skip the bottle entirely and get it looked at properly.

1. Try the safe basics first

A good plunger, a drain snake, or a kettle of hot (not boiling, on PVC) water and a squirt of dish soap will shift a lot of minor grease and soap clogs without a single chemical.

2. Stop if the water's not moving

If it's fully blocked or backing up, that's your cue to stop pouring things in. More product won't help, and it makes the professional clear harder. Read the signs of a blocked drain so you know what you're dealing with.

3. Get a camera down the line

A CCTV inspection shows exactly what's causing it and how far down — roots, a grease band, a cracked pipe — so you fix the right thing instead of guessing.

4. Clear it with a hydro jet

A 5000 PSI hydro jet scours the pipe clean — grease, roots and debris — far more thoroughly than any chemical, and with no damage to sound pipework.

That's the order I'd want for my own place: safe DIY on a slow drain, and camera-then-jet on a real blockage. If the camera turns up a broken pipe behind the clog, pipe relining repairs it from the inside without digging up the yard, and for sewage backing up or an after-hours emergency our emergency plumber service runs 24/7.
