The Signs Your Drain Has Collapsed

A blocked drain and a broken drain feel similar from inside the house — the difference is that one stays fixed after clearing and the other never does. Watch for this pattern:

🔁 It keeps blocking

Cleared last month, blocked again this month — in the same spot. Waste snags on broken pipe edges, so a damaged section re-blocks fast no matter how well it's cleared.

🐌 Never drains quite right

A deformed or partially collapsed pipe narrows the line, so drains stay slow and gurgly even between blockages.

👃 Sewage smells outside

Wastewater leaking from a broken pipe into the soil produces persistent smells along the pipe run — often strongest after you've run a lot of water.

🌱 Lush strips & damp patches

A tell-tale stripe of greener, faster-growing lawn — or a permanently damp patch — traces the leak. Your sewer is fertilising the garden.

🕳️ Sunken ground or paving

Escaping water washes away the soil around the pipe. Dips in the lawn, dropping pavers or cracking paths along the drain line are late-stage warnings.

🐀 Pest activity

Rats use broken sewer pipes as a highway. Unexplained rodent activity near the drain run sometimes shows up before anything else.

What Actually Breaks a Drain in Melbourne's East

Around the Ranges and eastern suburbs — Lilydale, Mooroolbark, Belgrave, Ferntree Gully — most homes built before the 1980s still run their sewage through earthenware (terracotta) pipes with a joint every 600mm. Every joint is an invitation: tree roots find the moisture, force their way in, and over the years crack and lever the pipe apart. Add reactive clay soil that swells and shrinks with the seasons, the odd heavy vehicle over a shallow line, and time does the rest.

That's why a root-blocked drain and a broken drain are usually the same story at different chapters. Roots got in because the pipe had an opening; every year they're left in, the opening gets bigger. Which is exactly why we run a camera after every main-line clear — cutting the roots out fixes today, but only the footage tells you whether the pipe will survive next year.

Confirming It: the CCTV Camera Inspection

Nobody should excavate a yard on a guess. A CCTV drain inspection (some people call it a sewer camera or drain camera inspection) travels the full line and shows the damage on screen: cracks, root intrusion, deformed pipe, dropped joints or full collapse. Combined with pipe locating, it pinpoints the damaged section's exact position and depth from the surface — so if any repair is needed, it's measured in metres, not maybes.

You get the footage and a written report. That report is what insurers accept as evidence, what settles landlord-vs-tenant questions (see who's responsible for a blocked drain), and what any honest repair quote should be based on.

Repair cost depends entirely on what the camera finds: how long the damaged section is, how deep it sits, and whether it can be relined or needs targeted excavation. Anyone quoting a collapsed-drain repair over the phone without footage is guessing — usually in their own favour.
