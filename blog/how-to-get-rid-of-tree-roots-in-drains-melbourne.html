Why Tree Roots Get Into Your Drain

Roots aren't trying to wreck your plumbing — they're just chasing what's inside it. A drain line carries water and nutrients, and in a dry Melbourne spring or summer that's exactly what a thirsty tree is hunting for. Where a pipe has a hairline crack, a loose rubber joint or a section that's shifted in the ground, a tiny feeder root finds the moisture leaking out, pushes in through the gap, and then thrives in the damp, nutrient-rich pipe. Once it's in, it fans out into a fine mat that catches everything flushed past it — fat, paper, grit — until the whole line chokes up.

Two things make this especially common around here. The first is the trees: the leafy suburbs of the Dandenong Ranges and the Yarra Valley are full of big established eucalypts, liquidambars, willows and the like, all with aggressive, water-seeking roots. The second is the pipes. A lot of older homes across Melbourne's east still run on earthenware or terracotta drains laid decades ago, jointed every metre or so and prone to cracking as the ground moves. Those joints are an open invitation. Our guide on the worst suburbs for tree roots in drains goes into where it bites hardest, and why spring is peak season for root intrusion explains the timing.

The tell-tale signs are a drain that keeps blocking in the same spot, a toilet that gurgles or is slow to clear, and gradually worsening drainage rather than a sudden stop. If that sounds familiar, our rundown of the signs of a blocked drain and why a drain keeps blocking in the same place will help you work out whether roots are the likely culprit.

Do Root Killers Actually Work?

This is the big question, so here's the straight answer: shop-bought root killers have their place, but not the one most people hope for. They can kill roots on contact and slow down regrowth for a while — which makes them a reasonable maintenance step after a drain has been properly cleared. What they can't do is remove a root mass that's already blocking the pipe, and they can't repair the crack that's letting roots in. Tip a product down a blocked drain and you'll mostly be treating standing water.

Product What it does The verdict
Copper sulfate crystalsKills roots on contact, leaves a residue that deters regrowthMaintenance only — won't clear a blockage; can harm the tree & soil and isn't suited to every system
Foaming root killerFoam coats the whole pipe wall, so it reaches roots above the water lineBetter coverage than crystals, but still only slows regrowth — doesn't remove the mass or fix the pipe
Rock salt / copper sulfate DIY mixesDehydrates roots over timeSlow, inconsistent, and heavy salt loads can corrode metal pipes and aren't septic-friendly
Baking soda / vinegarFizzes, shifts a little loose gunkDoes essentially nothing to roots — don't rely on it

So root killers aren't a scam, but they're oversold. Think of them as a way to keep regrowth down between proper services, not a cure. And a word of caution with copper sulfate in particular: it can damage the tree you're trying to keep, affect surrounding plants and soil, and it's not suitable for septic systems or some pipe materials. If you've got a septic tank or you're not sure what your pipes are made of, check before you pour anything down there — or just give me a call and I'll tell you over the phone.

How a Plumber Removes Tree Roots Properly

Clearing roots the right way is a two-part job: get them out, then find out why they got in. Here's how I do it.

1. Camera the line first

A CCTV drain inspection shows where the roots are, how bad the mass is, and the exact crack or joint they're coming through — so we fix the real problem instead of guessing.

2. Cut them out with a hydro jet

A 5000 PSI hydro jet with a cutting head shreds the root mass and scours the pipe wall clean — far more thoroughly than a drain machine, which tends to just punch a hole through the roots.

3. Camera again to check

A second camera pass confirms the line is clear and shows the condition of the pipe underneath — whether it's sound, cracked or in need of a repair.

4. Fix the entry point

If the camera finds a cracked or broken section, that's where the roots keep returning. Sealing it is the step that actually stops the cycle — more on that below.

Why not just a drain snake or an electric eel? Those tools will often clear enough roots to get the water moving again, and that's genuinely useful in an emergency. But a cutter on a cable tends to bore a channel straight through the root mat rather than strip it off the pipe wall, so the roots are still there and the line blocks again sooner. A hydro jet cleans the full bore of the pipe. The Only Permanent Fix: Repairing the Pipe

Here's the part that matters most, because it's where people waste the most money. If you only ever clear roots, you'll be clearing them forever — every year or two, the roots find the same gap and grow straight back. Clearing treats the symptom. To actually get rid of tree roots for good, you have to close the door they're coming through.

In most cases that means pipe relining: a new resin liner is cured inside the old pipe, forming a smooth, jointless, sealed pipe-within-a-pipe. There's no crack and no open joint left for roots to exploit, and because it's done through existing access points it usually means no digging up your yard, driveway or garden. For a badly collapsed section the fix may be a dig-and-replace instead — our guide on collapsed drain repair covers when that's the case. Either way, the principle is the same: fix the pipe, and the roots have nowhere to go.

This is also the honest reason I camera every main-line job before quoting a repair. Until we can see the pipe, no one can tell you truthfully whether you need a quick clear, a reline of one section, or something more — and I'd rather show you the footage and let you decide than sell you a fix you might not need.

How to Stop Them Coming Back

Once the line's clear and the pipe's sorted, a bit of upkeep keeps roots from ever becoming a problem again — especially if you've got big trees near the drain run.

🔧 Seal the weak point

The big one. Relining or replacing the cracked section removes the gap entirely — the single most effective thing you can do to stop roots for good.

💦 Maintenance jet

If relining isn't on the cards yet, a jet every year or two keeps regrowth in check and stops a small mat turning into a full blockage.

🌱 Plant with the drain in mind

Avoid thirsty, aggressive species right over the drain line, and consider a root barrier for new plantings near the pipe run.

📷 Yearly camera check

A quick CCTV look catches a new crack or early regrowth while it's cheap and easy to deal with, long before it backs up the house.

You'll notice removing the tree isn't top of that list. Nine times out of ten we fix the pipe and leave the garden alone — pulling out a mature tree is costly, often needs council approval across many Melbourne council areas, and doesn't even guarantee the problem's gone. The pipe is the fault, so the pipe is what we fix.
