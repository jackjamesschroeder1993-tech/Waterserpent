Glen Waverley Sits Under a Different Council to Its Neighbours

This page is written differently from the other suburb pages on this site for one reason: the rules here are not the same. Drive twenty minutes east and you are in Knox, or north and you are in Whitehorse, and both of those use different terminology, different authorities and different design standards. Glen Waverley belongs to the City of Monash, and the Monash Civic Centre is not somewhere else in the municipality — it is in the suburb itself, at 293 Springvale Road, Glen Waverley.

The suburb is also much larger than most people picture. It stretches from Blackburn Road across to Dandenong Creek, and from Highbury Road down to Ferntree Gully Road. Inside that footprint you have the terminus of the Glen Waverley railway line, The Glen shopping centre, the Kingsway restaurant strip, Central Reserve, Bogong Reserve and the Scotchmans Creek Trail. A blocked drain at the western end and a blocked drain near the creek are, in practice, two different pieces of the drainage story.

What all of it shares is a straightforward principle. Whatever the postcode, whatever the council, the pipes on your side of the boundary are yours to look after — and in Glen Waverley that boundary sits further out than most owners realise.

Monash Says Legal Point of Discharge, and Measures Storms in AEP

Every council in Melbourne has a phrase for the handover point between private and public drainage, and no two of them say it quite the same way. Knox calls it an Approved Point of Discharge. Maroondah calls it a point of discharge. Monash uses Legal Point of Discharge, usually abbreviated to LPOD. If you are searching council documents for guidance on a Glen Waverley property, that is the term to search for.

Monash is unusually direct about how far your responsibility extends, and this is the sentence that surprises Glen Waverley owners most often: "Property owners are responsible for the drains and pipes that collect stormwater on their property, including the pipes that run under the footpath and nature strip. These pipes and pits are part of your private stormwater system." The nature strip is not a grey area. It is your pipe.

It goes further still. Council states that "You are responsible for all private drains within and from your property until they connect to the Council's drainage network. In some circumstances these private drains may be in the street or through other properties." On subdivided or battle-axe blocks around Glen Waverley, a private line running through a neighbouring property is not a rarity.

🗺️ The single most useful thing Monash publishes: the Council openly states that it "has limited information on the location of drainage on residential and commercial properties, as plumbers are not required to inform Council of the location of these private drains when they install them." Read that twice. Nobody holds a map of your private drainage. Not the Council, not the water retailer, not the previous owner's conveyancer. If you need to know where the line runs before you build over it or dig near it, somebody has to physically trace and mark it.

The other Monash quirk is how storms are described. Most councils in the east quote drainage protection as an ARI, an average recurrence interval, so you hear about a 5 year or 10 year standard. Monash frames it as an AEP, an annual exceedance probability, which is the same idea expressed as a percentage chance in any given year. Its local drainage programme covers minor storms of less than 5% AEP, while a separate strategic programme handles major storms greater than 5% AEP. Practically, it means a Glen Waverley street can take water in a genuinely big storm without a single thing being blocked.

🌧️ Council stormwater — Monash

Monash "manages most of the stormwater drainage network to cope with rainwater in our local streets, but not private drains." Report a Council drain on 9518 3555. The stated response time is 10 working days, which tells you plainly that it is not an emergency service for water in your house.

🚽 Sewer — Yarra Valley Water

Glen Waverley falls in Yarra Valley Water's patch, on 13 27 62. Monash is a split municipality, and the Council directs residents to "either Yarra Valley Water on 13 2762 … or South East Water on 13 16 94" depending on the address. The Knox suburbs just east are South East Water, so check the retailer named on your bill.

🌊 Creeks and trunk drains — Melbourne Water

Council is explicit that "Melbourne Water is responsible for large drains and creeks." The Department of Transport looks after drainage on some arterial roads as well. So a creek-level event, a highway pit and a blocked yard drain are three separate conversations with three separate bodies.

Renting in Glen Waverley? Drainage is part of the building, so the cost of repairing it generally sits with the owner — unless it is obvious that something went down the pipe that never should have. Tenants get the best outcome by reporting it in writing so the request carries a date, and by photographing whatever is visible before anyone touches it. Owners and managers should ask for the camera recording, because footage is the only evidence that reliably separates a failed pipe from a misused one. The First Ten Minutes — What Actually Helps

Most of the damage from a blocked drain happens after the blockage, not because of it. These five steps keep it contained until we get there.

  1. Shut the water off at every fixture and leave it off. A blocked line has a fixed volume in it, and each extra litre pushes the water level up toward the lowest opening in the building.
  2. Cancel the appliance cycles. A washing machine or dishwasher pumping out mid-cycle delivers a large volume in one hit, and that is very often the moment a contained problem becomes a floor-covering problem.
  3. Go outside and locate the grated fitting nearest the house. That is the overflow relief gully, and it sits lower than the floors on purpose, so a surcharge is released into the garden rather than the hallway. If it is spilling it is working. Never build over it, never pot-plant on top of it.
  4. In a storm, clear the grates and check the downpipes. Lifting a mat of leaves off a surface pit takes two minutes and can be the difference between a wet path and water against the wall.
  5. Do not pour anything down it. Supermarket drain products achieve nothing against roots or set grease, and they leave a line full of caustic liquid that has to be dealt with before the real work can start.

The Glen Waverley Drain Calendar

Our callouts here follow the weather closely enough that you can plan around them.

🍂 Autumn

Gutters and surface pits load up faster than anyone expects on established Glen Waverley blocks. Clearing them in March costs an hour. Discovering they were full in June costs considerably more than that.

🌧️ Winter

Long, steady rain is the true test of a stormwater line, because it removes any margin a partly restricted pipe was relying on. This is when standing water and slab-edge dampness turn up on the phone.

🌱 Spring

Growth resumes underground. Any sewer that was cleared over winter without the entry point being sealed tends to close over again about now, and it is usually the same metre of pipe.

☀️ Summer

Sudden convective storms are the flash-flood risk in this catchment, and hard, dry ground sheds them rather than absorbing them. Kitchen lines also close down faster while fat is setting firmer.

Signs a Glen Waverley Drain Is In Trouble

Some of these get acted on within the hour. Others get lived with for a year before anyone joins the dots.

  • One fixture reacting when you use another — flush the toilet and hear the basin, run the bath and hear the shower. Our guide on gurgling drains explains the physics.
  • A puddle that reappears in exactly the same place after every rain — that repetition is the tell. Rare storms move around; a blocked line does not.
  • The outside grate lifting or spilling — the overflow relief gully doing its job. Treat it as urgent, not as a nuisance.
  • Damp showing at the base of a wall after storms — often a stormwater outlet that is no longer taking what the roof delivers.
  • A drain odour that shifts with the weather — see why drains smell.
  • The same fixture blocking on an annual cycle — that is a defect keeping an appointment, not bad luck.
  • A kitchen sink that has slowed a little each year — fat closing the branch from the wall inwards. See kitchen sink draining slowly.

How We Clear a Blocked Drain in Glen Waverley

Stormwater or sewer, branch or main, and where the line can be entered. Since nobody holds a plan of private drainage here, that first stage is often a locating job rather than a clearing one.

2️⃣ Choose the method to suit the material

Soft, local build-up comes out on a drain machine. Roots, storm silt, scale and hardened grease get 5000 PSI hydro blasting, which works outward against the pipe wall and restores the full bore.

3️⃣ Put the camera back through

With flow restored, the CCTV camera runs the same length again. That confirms the result and reads the condition of the barrel while there is nothing sitting in the way. The recording is yours to keep.

4️⃣ Explain what the footage shows

A fracture, an open joint, a belly holding water, a badly made connection. You get told straight, including the times when the honest advice is to leave it alone and watch it.

Repeat Blockages, and When Relining Beats Clearing

There is a threshold where clearing the same length of pipe stops being maintenance. If a Glen Waverley line has closed over twice in the same spot, the material that got in is a symptom and the opening it came through is the problem.

Relining deals with the opening. A resin-saturated liner is drawn into the damaged run, pressed hard against the existing pipe and cured in place, leaving one continuous internal wall where a chain of jointed sections used to be. Nothing above ground is opened up, which on the established Glen Waverley blocks we work on usually means a driveway, a crossover or a mature garden is left untouched.

It is not the answer to everything. A section that has genuinely collapsed, been crushed, or lost its fall so badly that water no longer travels wants replacing, and we will say so rather than sell you a liner. The footage is what decides it. See pipe relining and our comparison of relining versus replacement.

Four Cars in the Syndal Underpass — February 2011

Glen Waverley has a flash-flooding record, and it is documented well enough that you do not have to take anyone's word for it. The VICSES Monash Local Flood Guide, reviewed in November 2020, sets out both the geography and the events.

On the geography, the guide records that "Creeks that start in the City of Monash include Gardiners Creek, Scotchmans Creek and Valley Creek", and that "The Dandenong Creek on the eastern border of the Monash municipality is the only river with a flood warning system in place." Everything else in the municipality is a headwater. Water is generated here rather than arriving from somewhere upstream, which is exactly why the trouble takes the form of short, violent flash flooding rather than a slow river rise.

The locations the guide names for flash flooding will be familiar to anyone who drives the suburb: the rail underpass at Blackburn Road beside Syndal station, Springvale Road between The Boulevard and Aurish Avenue, Brandon Park Drive, the Dandenong Creek end of High Street, and Atkinson Street where it crosses Scotchmans Creek.

Then there is February 2011, which is the event locals still talk about. Monash SES took 185 requests for assistance. Four cars had to be pulled out of the Syndal underpass, and the water took about six hours to drain away. All eight lanes of the Princes Highway were blocked for roughly five hours. People were evacuated from their cars along Brandon Park Drive. That is what a headwater catchment looks like when a storm parks over it.

The Flood Maps Being Drawn Right Now

Here is the part that is live rather than historical, and it is worth knowing about if you own property in Glen Waverley.

Monash is running a Flood Mapping Project. Community feedback ran through March and April 2025, the flood modelling is due to be completed in August 2026, and the maps are to be published late in 2026. Melbourne Water is producing the modelling, using Australian Rainfall and Runoff 2019 methods together with climate forecasts extending to 2100. The Council is not doing this voluntarily — it is required to map flood-liable land under Regulation 148 of the Building Regulations 2018.

Two planning overlays come out the other end of that work, and both are worth checking against your address: the Special Building Overlay (SBO) and the Land Subject to Inundation Overlay (LSIO). If either lands on a property, it changes what can be built and how. Council has also been delivering physical works alongside the mapping, including a $330,000 litter trap at Bogong Reserve in Glen Waverley.

📋 Why a homeowner should care about a mapping project: because the maps describe the public system, not yours. If new modelling shows water travelling across your block in a big storm, the first practical question is whether your own stormwater line is running at full bore or at half of it. A silted pit and a root-bound outlet do not show up on a flood map — they show up on a camera. Getting the private side clear is the one part of that picture you actually control.

From Black Flat to a District Centre

Glen Waverley was originally called Black Flat. A school opened in 1868, and the name we use today settled in May 1905, drawn from Walter Scott's Waverley novels. The railway to Melbourne arrived in 1930, and for the next three decades this was still a modest settlement on the edge of the metropolitan area.

The Victorian Places project, run jointly by Monash University and the University of Queensland, records that "During the 1960s Glen Waverley underwent rapid residential growth". In 1974 the Council designated Glen Waverley a district centre, and the character of the place changed again: The Glen superseded the open-air Kingsway shops, with David Jones added in 1996. Much of the surrounding housing went in through private-sector estates, A.V. Jennings being the most prominent developer, including a Robin Boyd-designed estate near Brentwood and the earlier Appletree Hill.

The census numbers make the pace obvious: 238 residents in 1911, 509 in 1947, 36,591 in 2001 and 39,204 in 2011. A suburb that adds that many people that quickly does not get one generation of drainage — it gets several, laid to whatever the standard of the day happened to be, and then progressively built over.

That is the practical consequence for a plumber. Two houses in the same Glen Waverley street can differ by decades in pipe material, in depth, in where the inspection opening sits and in whether the original stormwater outlet was ever properly connected. Nobody can read that from the surface, which is why the camera goes down before the machine does.

The Suburban Rail Loop and More Roof on an Older Network

Glen Waverley is one of the precincts named in the Suburban Rail Loop East programme, and the Victorian Government fact sheet for the suburb makes clear this is a long horizon rather than a short one. The Conceptual Precinct Plan "identifies where longer-term change is expected to occur into the 2050s", keeping the higher change along Springvale Road, Waverley Road and High Street Road and retaining Kingsway as the retail strip. Planning scheme amendments are to be gazetted in 2026 if approved.

Set aside the politics of it. From a drainage point of view, densification does one thing above all: it replaces garden with roof, driveway and paving. Every square metre that stops absorbing rain starts shedding it, and all of that extra water is directed into a private stormwater system that was sized for a single house on a large block, discharging into a public network laid decades earlier.

That mismatch is already the most common reason we get called to Glen Waverley addresses that never had a problem before. A rear townhouse goes in, or a large extension and a paved entertaining area, and suddenly the old outlet pipe is being asked to carry two or three times its original load. It usually copes until the first serious storm, and then it does not.

If you are planning work on a Glen Waverley block, the sensible order is to establish where the existing drainage runs and what condition it is in before the design is locked in. It is a modest job at that stage and a genuinely disruptive one afterwards.
