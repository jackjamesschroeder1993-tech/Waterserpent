Five Things Worth Doing Before We Get There

Whether it is a backed-up sewer or water building up outside during a storm, these buy you time and limit the damage.

  1. Cut off everything that puts water in. Taps off, and stop the washing machine and dishwasher mid-cycle if you have to. Both discharge a large slug in one go, and a stopped line has to put that somewhere.
  2. Resist the confirming flush. The flush people use to check whether a toilet has cleared itself is the one that usually reaches the floor.
  3. Get outside and look at the grated fitting near the house. That is the overflow relief gully, deliberately set below floor level so a surcharge escapes into the garden and not down the hallway. If it is bubbling it is doing its job. Never build over it, pave it in or stand pot plants on it.
  4. If rain is the trigger, walk the surface drainage. Lift the grates you can reach, pull the leaf mat off, and check whether downpipes are actually discharging into their pits rather than beside them. On a sloping Ferntree Gully block that two-minute check can stop water reaching the building.
  5. Skip the drain chemicals. They will not shift roots, compacted silt or hardened grease, and all they achieve is leaving a caustic pool for whoever opens the line next.

The Ferntree Gully Year — A Warm-Season Flood Risk

Most drainage advice assumes winter is the dangerous season. Here the risk profile is tilted differently, and it is worth planning around.

🌱 Spring

Flash flooding season begins, according to the local flood guide, and root growth resumes at the same time. A sewer cleared over winter starts closing again if the gap the roots came through was never sealed. Double exposure, and the busiest quarter of our year in 3156.

☀️ Summer

The other half of the flash-flood window. Hard, dry ground sheds a sudden storm instead of absorbing it, so the whole burst arrives at the pits at once. Kitchen waste lines also narrow faster while there is less water going through them.

🍂 Autumn

The maintenance window. Leaf loading peaks and, on sloping ground, it does not stay where it lands. This is the cheapest month of the year to have gutters, grates and pits emptied and a stormwater line checked properly.

❄️ Winter

Long soaking rain rather than sharp bursts. It is less likely to overwhelm the system outright, but it finds every line running below capacity and keeps finding it for weeks, which is when slow yard drainage becomes obvious.

The term Knox uses for that last handover point is worth learning, because it appears on permits, plans and council correspondence. Knox puts it this way: every property is given a point to direct its stormwater runoff, and this is known as an Approved Point of Discharge, or APD. Downpipes, surface grates, yard pits and the pipework connecting them to the APD all belong to the property. What sits past that point does not.

Knox is also unusually direct about what it will and will not do beyond that. Its published position, in one sentence, is that household drainage infrastructure is not something the council carries a legal responsibility to keep in repair. There is no ambiguity there, and it is worth absorbing before lodging a request in the expectation that a crew will arrive with a jetter. If your own stormwater line is silted or root-bound, that is a plumbing job.

Finally, the design standards. Knox provides flood protection to a 5-year ARI level in residential areas and a 10-year ARI level in commercial ones, while Melbourne Water takes on catchments above 60 hectares and works to a 10-year through 100-year ARI range. Those are deliberate engineering choices, not failures. But they mean a Ferntree Gully street can carry water briefly in a genuinely big storm with nothing whatsoever blocked. The line that matters to you as an owner is this: if your property holds water in ordinary rain, that is not the network being exceeded, that is your own drainage being restricted.

Signs a Ferntree Gully Drain Is Heading for Trouble

Some of these send people straight to the phone. Others get lived with for a year before anyone connects them to a pipe.

  • Noise passing between fixtures — empty the bath and a basin gulps back at you, or flush and hear the floor waste answer. See why drains gurgle.
  • Water arriving somewhere new during rain — under a deck, along a retaining wall, or across a garage slab. On sloping ground this is usually redirection from a blocked pit, not rising groundwater.
  • The yard gully spilling — your overflow relief gully is surcharging outside so it does not surcharge inside. Urgent.
  • A downpipe that overshoots its pit in heavy rain — either the pit is full or the line below it is restricted. Easy to see, easy to ignore.
  • The same drain closing over on a schedule — each spring, or after every heavy storm. That is a defect keeping an appointment, not bad luck.
  • Odour that comes and goes with the weather — see why drains smell.
  • The kitchen sink losing a little more speed each month — cooled fat closing the bore from the wall inwards. See kitchen sink draining slowly.

When Clearing Becomes a Subscription — Relining in Ferntree Gully

There is a point at which opening the same line again is not maintenance. If a drain here has closed over twice in the same location, the pipe is the problem and the material inside it is just the symptom that keeps announcing it.

Relining repairs the run from the inside, with nothing dug up. A resin-saturated liner is drawn through the damaged length, pressed hard against the host pipe and cured in place, so an open socket, a hairline split and a rough patch of wall all end up behind one continuous new surface with nothing to catch on and nowhere for roots to re-enter. On the terraced, retained and heavily planted blocks that are typical of the streets below the ranges, that matters more than usual — excavating a line on a slope frequently means dismantling retaining work and landscaping that cost far more than the pipe did.

It is not the answer to everything. Where a section has genuinely collapsed, been crushed, or lost its fall so badly that water will not run, replacement is the honest recommendation and we will say so. The camera decides that, not a guess. See pipe relining, and our side-by-side look at relining versus replacement.

5 February 2011 — 140 Millimetres in Twelve Hours

Ferntree Gully has something almost no other suburb in Knox has: its own dedicated local flood guide. VICSES publishes a guide titled Upper Ferntree Gully at Knox, with content reviewed in July 2025, rather than folding the suburb into a shared district document the way it does for most of the municipality. That on its own tells you the flood risk here is considered specific enough to deserve its own treatment.

The guide's account of the worst recorded event is blunt. It records that "The 5 February, 2011 flood event was one of the most significant in the area. Upper Ferntree Gully received a massive 140 millimetres of rainfall in just 12 hours." It also sets out the general rule for the area: "Moderate to heavy rainfall over a period of 12 hours or more usually results in severe flooding in the area."

What happened on the ground that day is listed road by road. Closures included Burwood Highway at Hilltop Road, Upper Ferntree Gully; Burwood Highway at Station Street, Ferntree Gully; Brenock Park Drive; Forest Road at the Railway Underpass; and Mountain Gate Drive. Ferntree Plaza Shopping Centre appears in the guide as community infrastructure likely to flood.

Read those names together and a pattern falls out. They are the low points: highway dips, an underpass, and the crossings where a main road meets a drainage line. Water in this suburb does not spread out slowly, it collects at the bottom of things. Your own block obeys exactly the same logic on a smaller scale — the lowest opening on the property is where a stopped line will show itself, which is usually a shower base indoors and the overflow relief gully outdoors.

📋 Roads listed as likely to flood: the same guide names Acacia Road, Burwood Highway, Clow Avenue, Dawson Street, Forest Oak Drive, Mount View Road, New Road, Rollings Road and Waters Avenue. If your property sits on one of these, or downhill of one, the case for keeping your own pits, grates and downpipe lines genuinely clear is stronger than it would be almost anywhere else in Knox. Living Below the Ranges — Why Runoff Here Arrives All at Once

Ferntree Gully sits at the foot of the Dandenongs, and the topography does more to shape drainage calls here than the age of the housing does. Burwood Highway and Ferntree Gully Road converge at Mountain Gate; the land climbs east from there through Upper Ferntree Gully, where the 1000 Steps and Kokoda Track Memorial Walk leaves the Ferntree Gully Picnic Area at Mount Dandenong Tourist Road and Burwood Highway, and drops away west toward Scoresby Road, which forms the suburb's western boundary. That is a lot of vertical difference inside one postcode.

Steep ground does two things to a drainage system. First, it delivers water fast: rain that falls on the upper streets is at the bottom of the catchment in minutes rather than hours, so a pipe that copes fine with a long steady drizzle can be overrun by a short violent burst. Second, fast water carries material. Grit, sand, leaf litter and garden mulch are picked up on the slopes and dropped the moment the grade flattens out, which is why the pits that silt up worst are rarely the ones at the top of the hill.

There is a timing quirk here too that catches people out. The flood guide notes that flash flooding in this area "usually occurs in spring and summer" — which is the opposite of the assumption most homeowners work from. Winter rain is what everyone braces for, but the events that put water through a Ferntree Gully doorway have tended to be the sharp warm-season downpours landing on ground that has already dried and hardened off. Practically, that means the sensible time to have a stormwater line checked is not the first cold week of June. It is well before it.

The other half of the picture is what happens above the surface. Steep blocks are usually terraced, retained and landscaped, which means surface water gets channelled deliberately, and any pit or agricultural line built into that landscaping is now part of your drainage system whether or not anyone still remembers installing it. On a slope, a single blocked pit does not just pond — it redirects, and the water finds the next lowest thing, which is frequently a garage slab or the underside of a deck.

If you are on Wrexham Road, Stephen Road or the streets feeding them, it is worth lifting your own grates once or twice over that period rather than waiting to discover them full. It also reinforces something worth repeating: South East Water is the water and sewerage authority for this part of Knox, not Yarra Valley Water. Their number is 132 812.

How Ferntree Gully Was Built, and What That Left Underground

The bones of the suburb go back a long way. A school was operating by 1880, and the Shire of Fern Tree Gully was proclaimed on 30 May 1889, covering 390 square kilometres and a population of about 750. The railway reached here the same year, extended from Ringwood through to Upper Ferntree Gully, and was electrified in 1929. The national park was declared in 1927. Census counts show how slowly things moved at first: 766 people in 1891, about 1,000 in 1933, then 1,947 in 1947, according to the Victorian Places project run by Monash University and the University of Queensland.

Then the post-war decades arrived and the pace changed completely. A kindergarten opened in 1949, a boys' technical school and a second primary school both in 1954. By the 2011 Census the population was 25,585. One detail from that era is specific to this suburb and worth flagging: in 1954 the State Government enacted planning controls over excessive subdivision here. Ferntree Gully was being cut up faster than services could follow, and the response was regulatory.

That history is not trivia when you are standing over an open inspection opening. It means a single Ferntree Gully street can hold pipework from several different eras sitting side by side, with different materials, different depths and different connection details. Older jointed drainage is assembled from short sections with a socket every metre or so, and each socket is a place where ground movement can open a gap. Newer work is more often continuous plastic. Neither is a safe assumption, which is precisely why we put a camera in rather than an opinion.
